# Hot-start optimizer study — results, 2026-08-02

Archive: `reproduce/outputs/opt-hotcold-2026-08-02/`
Protocol: Concrete, 2nd-order consequents, 144 antecedent parameters, 10 seeds,
2,000 objective evaluations per arm per seed, trust-region radius 1.0 (the full
box), single-threaded, budget enforced by a wrapper that raises.

The study asks two questions. How much can an optimizer improve the fuzzy model
in a fixed number of iterations, and how much of that depends on starting from
the Gaussian construction rather than from nowhere.

---

## The short version

1. **The construction's parameter *placement* is worth nothing measurable here.**
   A uniform random draw from the same box scores as well as the fitted model
   before any optimization: paired **+0.010 ± 0.033 R², random winning 6/10
   seeds**.
2. **What the construction is worth, in iterations, is about three.** A
   population method starting from a random point matches the construction's
   objective in 2–3 evaluations. A local method needs ~250.
3. **No optimizer is separable from any other.** Every arm-vs-arm paired
   difference in held-out R² is smaller than its own spread.
4. **The objective everything is optimized against does not track
   generalization.** Three independent signs of it, below.

Point 1 has a load-bearing qualification, and it is the first thing to read.

---

## What "cold" does and does not randomize

A cold run draws its antecedents uniformly from `build_param_bounds(model,
X_train)`. Those bounds put each centre inside its feature's observed range and
each width inside a sensible fraction of that range — and, critically, the
*shape* of the parameter vector comes from the fitted model: which features were
retained, how many mixture components sit on each, for which output bucket.

So a cold start inherits everything the construction discovered about
**structure** and randomizes only **placement and width**. This experiment
therefore decomposes the construction's contribution and measures one half of
it:

| what the construction supplies | measured here? | worth |
|---|---|---|
| structure — which features, how many components | **no** | untested |
| placement and width of each component | yes | ≈ 0.00 R² |

The untested half is what `run_structure_study.py` exists for. Until that lands,
nothing here licenses the sentence "the Gaussian construction is unnecessary".

---

## 1. Before any optimization

The `none` arm at zero evaluations — just scoring the starting point.

| starting point | test R² | CV MSE |
|---|---:|---:|
| Gaussian construction | 0.8408 ± 0.0338 | 0.00639 |
| random draw, same box | 0.8511 ± 0.0229 | 0.00619 |

Paired, random − construction: **+0.0103 ± 0.0325 R²**, random better on 6/10
seeds; **−0.00020 ± 0.00056 CV MSE**, random better on 5/10. Indistinguishable
in both directions, which is the point: the construction is not *ahead*.

The likely mechanism is that the consequents are re-solved in closed form for
whatever antecedents are present. Given a partition of the input space that is
merely sensible — in range, sensibly scaled — the ridge-TSK solve appears to
absorb the difference. That is a claim about this dataset at this consequent
order and should be read as one.

## 2. The price of not having the construction

Evaluations before a cold run's objective matched what the construction supplies
for free (mean ± s.d. over 10 seeds):

| arm | evaluations |
|---|---:|
| scipy-de | 2 ± 2 |
| opt-ga | 3 ± 8 |
| opt-pso | 3 ± 8 |
| opt-aco | 3 ± 8 |
| scipy-powell | 239 ± 371 |
| scipy-lbfgsb | 272 ± 303 |
| opt-gd | 272 ± 303 |

The split is between population and local methods, and it is large. A population
method draws a spread of candidates immediately and one of them is already at
the construction's level; a local method has to walk there, and with 144
parameters a single finite-difference gradient costs ~144 evaluations, so ~250
is roughly two gradient steps.

Read as "what is the construction worth", the answer at this budget is **two to
three evaluations if you are going to run a population method afterwards**.

## 3. After the full budget

At 2,000 evaluations, held-out R², and the paired gain over the construction:

| arm | hot R² | cold R² | hot Δ vs heuristic | cold Δ vs heuristic |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| scipy-lbfgsb | 0.854 ± 0.044 | 0.869 ± 0.020 | +0.013 ± 0.029 | +0.028 ± 0.038 |
| scipy-powell | 0.861 ± 0.028 | 0.866 ± 0.027 | +0.020 ± 0.041 | +0.025 ± 0.040 |
| scipy-de | 0.863 ± 0.022 | 0.863 ± 0.022 | +0.022 ± 0.036 | +0.022 ± 0.036 |
| opt-ga | 0.875 ± 0.021 | 0.869 ± 0.025 | +0.034 ± 0.023 | +0.028 ± 0.032 |
| opt-pso | 0.867 ± 0.020 | 0.871 ± 0.019 | +0.026 ± 0.022 | +0.030 ± 0.031 |
| opt-aco | 0.872 ± 0.015 | 0.872 ± 0.015 | +0.030 ± 0.032 | +0.031 ± 0.031 |
| opt-gd | 0.854 ± 0.044 | 0.867 ± 0.024 | +0.013 ± 0.029 | +0.026 ± 0.038 |

Two things to notice. The cold column is not worse — for the two local methods
it is *better* (L-BFGS-B 0.869 cold against 0.854 hot). And every arm improves
on the construction by +0.013 to +0.034, on 6–10 seeds out of 10.

**No ordering survives pairing.** GA against L-BFGS-B, hot, is +0.020 ± 0.040
with 7/10 seeds won; every other pair is smaller relative to its spread. The
ranking in the table above is not a result and must not be quoted as one.

## 4. The objective is not tracking generalization

Three independent sightings in this one study:

- **Conversion rates differ ~3.6×.** L-BFGS-B removes 31% of the CV objective
  and gains +0.004 R² per 10% removed; PSO removes 17% and gains +0.015 per 10%.
  The arms that drive the objective hardest bank the least of it.
- **Hot L-BFGS-B beats cold L-BFGS-B on the objective and loses on R²** (0.00445
  vs 0.00515 CV; 0.854 vs 0.869 R²).
- **A cold start needs 272 evaluations to match the construction's objective but
  is already level with it on test R² at zero.**

This is §6.3.5's overfitting-the-cross-validation-estimate mechanism, and here it
points at the *local* methods rather than the population ones.

---

## What this does and does not say about §6.3.5

§6.3.5 says a plain local optimizer usually beats the population methods, and
that a global search has very little left to find.

- **"L-BFGS-B beats the population methods": not supported here.** Also not
  reversed — the paired differences are noise. The honest status is that the
  ordering is unresolved.
- **"Very little left to find": in tension.** +0.02 to +0.03 R² on a 0.841
  baseline is small but consistent, found by every arm on most seeds.
- **Neither point is refuted, because this is a different regime.** §6.3.5's DE
  arm runs `maxiter=40, popsize=8` over 144 parameters — roughly 46,000
  evaluations against this study's 2,000. Twenty-three times the budget. The
  chapter's text should not be edited on this evidence; the budget sweep should
  be run first.

## Next, in order

1. **The structure study** (`run_structure_study.py`, built, not yet run). It
   measures the half of the construction's contribution this study could not.
2. **Widen the box.** If placement is worth nothing inside
   `build_param_bounds`, the natural question is where it starts to matter.
   Loosening the width band and re-running cold isolates scale from placement.
3. **Match §6.3.5's budget** (~46,000 evaluations) before touching Chapter 6's
   text.
4. **A second dataset.** Everything here is Concrete at one consequent order.

# Addendum — identification: the old way against the new one

Archive: `reproduce/outputs/opt-identification-2026-08-02/`
Concrete, 3 seeds, rule counts {2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12}, median of 3 repeats,
**single-threaded** (OMP/BLAS pinned before numpy imports), k-means `n_init=10`.

Both routes are asked for the same number of rules at every point. The rule
count is an input to the classical route and normally an output of the
construction, so comparing a 3-rule construction against a 9-rule classical
model would compare capacity rather than identification.

## What was found

**On cost the result is not close, and it is not in the construction's favour.**

| rules | construction | classical k-means | classical FCM |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 3 | 471 ± 4 ms | 12 ± 1 ms | 7 ± 1 ms |
| 8 | 1172 ± 27 ms | 22 ± 3 ms | 39 ± 2 ms |
| 12 | 1743 ± 32 ms | 26 ± 2 ms | 69 ± 4 ms |

Between **25× and 84× faster**, at every rule count, with spreads of a few
milliseconds. The construction's time goes on its $O(M^2)$ feature screening and
on fitting an automatically-chosen number of mixture components per (feature,
bucket); the classical route runs one clustering and projects it.

**On accuracy the classical route is not worse, and probably slightly better —
but three seeds cannot settle it.** Paired at matched rule count, pooled over
all six counts and three seeds (n = 18):

| | Δ R² vs construction | wins |
|---|---:|---:|
| classical k-means | +0.022 ± 0.075 | 13/18 |
| classical FCM | +0.026 ± 0.057 | 13/18 |

Both means are smaller than their own spreads. Individual rule counts at c = 4
and c = 6 fall outside spread in the classical route's favour, but at three
seeds that is weak evidence and should not be quoted as an ordering.

**On parameters the gap is arithmetic rather than statistical.** At matched rule
count the construction carries about three times the free antecedent parameters
— 142 against 48 at c = 3, 467 against 192 at c = 12 — because it fits several
mixture components per (feature, bucket) where the classical route places
exactly one Gaussian per (feature, rule). So panel (a)'s rough parity is
achieved by the classical route with a third of the capacity.

## What this does not establish

- **One dataset, and a small one.** Concrete is 824 × 8 after the split.
  k-means on that costs nothing; the construction's cost is dominated by fitting
  candidate mixtures rather than by row count. The two scale differently and the
  ordering could move on a large problem — which makes PhiUSIIL (235K × 54) the
  test that could overturn this, not merely a bigger example.
- **Three seeds.** Enough for the timing, which has millisecond spreads. Not
  enough for the accuracy comparison.
- **The construction picks its own rule count; the classical route is told.**
  Sweeping c is what makes the comparison fair, but it also hands the classical
  route a choice the construction makes for itself, and that is worth something
  the accuracy column does not show.
- **Nothing here is about the optimizer.** This is identification only. What an
  optimizer does afterwards, from either starting point, is the study above.

## Where that leaves the thesis claim

The expectation going in was that the construction would beat k-means and FCM
handily on time and be solid on initial accuracy. On this dataset the second
half holds only in the restricted placement comparison (`clusterinit`, where the
construction is ahead by +0.008 against k-means and +0.022 against FCM at ten
seeds), and the first half is contradicted outright by the identification sweep.

The honest reading is that the construction's advantage is not speed of
identification, and on Concrete it is not initial accuracy either. What it does
supply, which none of the comparisons above credit it for, is a rule count and a
feature set derived from the data rather than chosen. Whether that is worth its
cost is a question this study has not yet asked.

# Addendum 2 — PhiUSIIL: the same contest at scale, and it crosses over

> **Superseded by Addendum 3.** The numbers below stand as measured, but the
> *mechanism* this addendum gives for them is wrong: the flat cost curve is a
> 20,000-row subsampling cap inside `fit_gaussians`, not sublinear scaling. Kept
> as written, with the correction recorded rather than edited away.

Archive: `reproduce/outputs/opt-phishing-2026-08-02/`
PhiUSIIL, binary, top 10 of 50 features, 3 components per class, one seed,
median of 3 repeats, single-threaded.

**Timing here is model training only.** Feature engineering — the O(M²) screen
that ranks the 50 features and keeps 10 — is reported in its own table and
charged to neither route. Every route consumes the same selected feature set, so
folding the screen into whichever route happens to implement it would compare a
pipeline against a training step. It is not small on this dataset and it is not
hidden; it is simply not part of the answer to "which way of training a rule
base is faster".

## Training cost

| train rows | construction | k-means | FCM | vs k-means | vs FCM |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 4,000 | 430 ms | 18 ms | 67 ms | 24× | 6.4× |
| 16,000 | 1,017 ms | 51 ms | 216 ms | 20× | 4.7× |
| 40,000 | 1,704 ms | 106 ms | 389 ms | 16× | 4.4× |
| 96,000 | 1,901 ms | 253 ms | 958 ms | 7.5× | 2.0× |
| 188,636 | **2,008 ms** | 558 ms | **2,211 ms** | 3.6× | **0.91×** |

**The construction overtakes FCM**, at 188,636 rows, by 9%. That is an observed
crossover, not an extrapolation. Against k-means the gap narrows from 24× to
3.6× and is still open.

The mechanism is the slope. The construction's training grows **4.7× for 47× the
data** and is nearly flat above 40,000 rows (1,704 → 1,901 → 2,008 ms); a
clustering grows about linearly. Separating the feature engineering is what made
this visible: the screen grows linearly and was 55% of the construction's
reported total at the largest size, so bundling the two made the construction
look linear when its training is not.

## Feature engineering, for completeness

Shared by every route, charged to none, and it is the larger cost at scale:

| train rows | screening |
|---:|---:|
| 4,000 | 79 ms |
| 96,000 | 1,201 ms |
| 188,636 | 2,361 ms |

At full size the screen costs more than any route's training. If feature
selection is on the critical path for a deployment, it — not the rule
identification — is what to optimize.

## Accuracy

| train rows | construction | k-means | FCM |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 4,000 | 1.0000 | 0.9930 | 0.9950 |
| 96,000 | 0.9998 | 0.9963 | 0.9962 |
| 188,636 | 0.9998 | 0.9960 | 0.9959 |

Accuracy is the wrong axis on a saturated dataset — Chapter 4 says as much about
PhiUSIIL. As error rates it is **0.02% against 0.40%: twenty times fewer
mistakes**, and the construction gets there with **48 membership functions
against 60**, because it chooses its own component count per (feature, class)
while the classical route is given three everywhere.

## What this settles, and what it does not

- **It bounds the Concrete result rather than overturning it.** Classical
  identification is far cheaper on small problems; the advantage decays with n
  and, against FCM, is gone by ~190,000 rows.
- **It supports the accuracy half of the thesis claim**, which Concrete did not.
- **One seed.** Enough for a timing trend taken as a median of repeats. Thin for
  the accuracy column, though a 20× error ratio is unlikely to be noise.
- **Component counts are not matched** — classical got three per class, the
  construction chose fewer. `--pin-components` exists and has not been run.
- **Identification only.** No optimizer runs in this study.

## Next

1. **`--pin-components`**, so the parameter counts match exactly.
2. **Multi-seed** on the accuracy column.
3. **Past 190k rows**, to see how far the k-means gap closes.

# Addendum 3 — matched comparisons, and a retraction

Archives: `opt-phishing-2026-08-03/` (PhiUSIIL, 3 seeds, capped controls),
`opt-phishing-pinned-2026-08-03/` (same, component-pinned),
`opt-identification-pinned-2026-08-03/` (Concrete, component-pinned).
Figures: `fig_08_identification`, `fig_09_phishing_scaling`.

Addendum 2 ended with three named next steps — pin the component count, add
seeds, push past 190k rows. Doing the first two turned up something neither was
looking for, so this addendum starts with the correction.

## Retraction: the flat curve is a subsampling cap

Addendum 2 read the construction's near-flat cost above 40,000 rows as
sublinear scaling — "grows 4.7× for 47× the data" — and treated the FCM
crossover as evidence of a better algorithm. Reading `fit_gaussians` says
otherwise:

```python
def fit_gaussians(X, y, column, label_value, n_gaussians=0, max_samples=20_000):
    series = X[column][y == label_value].dropna()
    ...
    data = data[:max_samples]          # <-- every fit sees at most 20,000 rows
```

Above 20,000 rows per (feature, class) the construction's fitting cost **stops
growing by design**. What still scales is the boolean mask that extracts the
column — cheap, linear, and the entire remaining slope. The classical routes, by
contrast, cluster every row they are given. So the comparison in Addendum 2 was
a capped route against uncapped ones, and the crossover is what that asymmetry
looks like, not what better scaling looks like.

The control is to give the classical routes the same cap (`--cap-classical
20000`), and they flatten exactly as predicted:

| train rows | k-means, all rows | k-means, capped | FCM, all rows | FCM, capped |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 40,000 | 113 ms | 99 ms | 465 ms | 438 ms |
| 96,000 | 270 ms | 111 ms | 1,021 ms | 499 ms |
| 188,636 | 554 ms | **123 ms** | 2,590 ms | **405 ms** |

Below the cap the paired columns agree to within noise, which is the sanity
check that the cap is what is being measured. Above it, both capped routes go
flat. **Nothing about the construction's placement rule produces the flat
curve.** It is subsampling, it is a legitimate design choice, and it is not an
algorithmic scaling property — and Addendum 2 should not have called it one.

## Most of the construction's cost is model selection, not placement

The second finding came from `--pin-components`. With an automatic component
count, `find_optimal_gaussians` fits **four EM mixtures per (feature, class)**
to pick a count by BIC, then discards them and runs k-means at the winner. Pin
the count and that search disappears:

| train rows | construction, auto | construction, pinned | share that is BIC |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 4,000 | 407 ± 10 ms | 53 ± 4 ms | 87% |
| 40,000 | 1,776 ± 67 ms | 161 ± 3 ms | 91% |
| 188,636 | 2,042 ± 41 ms | 372 ± 6 ms | **82%** |

Same finding on Concrete, at every rule count: pinning to one Gaussian per
(feature, bucket) removes about 92% of the construction's identification time
and lands its parameter count **exactly** on the classical route's.

| rules | free params | construction, auto | construction, pinned | k-means | FCM |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 2 | 32 | 320 ms | 25.7 ms | 8.8 ms | 3.9 ms |
| 4 | 64 | 600 ms | 45.5 ms | 14.1 ms | 19.7 ms |
| 8 | 128 | 1,103 ms | 82.9 ms | 18.3 ms | 37.5 ms |
| 12 | 192 | 1,561 ms | 118.3 ms | 24.8 ms | 63.5 ms |

(The auto rows carry 99–467 parameters at these rule counts, so their timing was
never a matched comparison either.)

**The Concrete headline needs restating.** "Classical identification is 23–84×
cheaper" was two effects at once: rule placement, and a model-selection search
the classical route is never asked to perform. At matched parameter counts the
classical route is **1.9–6.6× cheaper** — still a clear win, and a different
claim.

## Where the matched comparison actually lands

PhiUSIIL at 188,636 training rows, everything matched — same rule count, same
parameter budget, same feature set, same prediction path, and the classical
routes given the construction's own 20,000-row cap:

| route | train | test accuracy | membership fns |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| k-means, capped | **123 ms** | 0.9941 ± 0.0030 | 60 |
| construction, pinned | 372 ms | **0.9997 ± 0.0000** | 43 |
| FCM, capped | 405 ms | 0.9960 ± 0.0002 | 60 |
| k-means, all rows | 554 ms | 0.9951 ± 0.0015 | 60 |
| construction, auto | 2,042 ms | 0.9997 ± 0.0000 | 48 |
| FCM, all rows | 2,590 ms | 0.9960 ± 0.0002 | 60 |

Read as errors rather than accuracy, because PhiUSIIL saturates: **3 errors per
10,000 against 40–59**, a 13–20× difference, with fewer membership functions.
That gap is stable across all three seeds and across both the pinned and the
automatic construction, and it is the one thing in this addendum that survives
every control applied to it.

On cost the honest summary is a tie, not a win: the pinned construction sits
between capped k-means (3× cheaper) and capped FCM (8% dearer). The 21% win over
uncapped FCM at full size is real as measured and explained by the cap.

## Concrete and PhiUSIIL still disagree, and that is the result

| | Concrete (824 × 8, regression) | PhiUSIIL (235,795 × 50, binary) |
|---|---|---|
| cost, matched | classical 1.9–6.6× cheaper | within 3× either way |
| accuracy, matched | indistinguishable at 3 seeds | construction 13–20× fewer errors |

The claim the proposal can support is **not** "construction beats clustering".
It is: *at matched parameter counts the construction costs the same order of
magnitude as classical identification, and on the larger, higher-dimensional
problem it is far more accurate; on a small low-dimensional one it is neither
better nor worse than a clustering, and it costs a few times more.* Both halves
are measured, both are reproducible, and the second half is what makes the first
credible.

## Two library observations, for the record

Neither is a bug, both are load-bearing and neither is documented at the call
site:

1. `fit_gaussians` silently truncates at 20,000 samples. A caller reading
   "fit Gaussians to this column" does not expect a sample cap, and any scaling
   claim made without knowing about it is wrong in the way Addendum 2 was.
2. `find_optimal_gaussians` throws away the four GMMs it fits, keeping only the
   component count, and the k-means that follows re-does the work. The BIC
   winner's own means are already available and would make the k-means
   redundant. Cheapest available speedup in the identification path by a wide
   margin — 82–91% of training time on these two datasets.

## Next, in order

1. **Widen the sigma band and re-run cold** — still open from the main study,
   and unrelated to any of this.
2. **Budget sweep to ~46,000 evaluations** before touching §6.3.5's numbers.
3. **Multi-seed accuracy past 190k rows**, now that the cap is understood: the
   interesting question is whether the construction's error advantage survives
   when the classical routes get data it never sees.

# Addendum 4 — the library was the finding

Archives: `opt-identification-kmbic-2026-08-03/`,
`opt-identification-kmbic-pinned-2026-08-03/`, `opt-phishing-kmbic-2026-08-03/`,
`opt-phishing-kmbic-pinned-2026-08-03/`, `opt-phishing-kmbic-capped-2026-08-03/`.
Library: `tribble-fis` branch `claude/identification-cost-fix`, commit `10205df`,
rationale in `tribble-fis/docs/identification-cost-evaluation.md`.

Addendum 3 named two defects in `fit_gaussians` and left them standing, because
the study's job was to measure, not to patch. Both are now fixed, and the
identification result they were distorting **reverses**.

## What was fixed

1. **The 20,000-row cap** — applied unconditionally, taken as a *prefix* rather
   than a sample, and invisible from `create_gaussian_membership_dict`. Now
   `max_samples=None` by default, exposed, and a random draw when set.
2. **Fit-select-discard-refit** — `find_optimal_gaussians` fitted a full EM
   mixture at every candidate count, kept the winning integer, discarded four
   mixtures, and let `fit_gaussians` run *another* k-means at that count. Now
   each candidate is scored straight off the k-means partition it implies, and
   the winner's components are what ship.

Placement is untouched. Where the two selectors agree on a component count the
memberships are identical to the last digit, and at one component per (feature,
bucket) — where there is no selection to do — the pinned Concrete run reproduces
the old R² and CV MSE **exactly**, at 3.9× the speed. So the pinned rows below
are a pure timing change against a byte-identical model.

## The Concrete headline reverses

Matched parameter counts, three seeds, median of three repeats, single-threaded.
Identification only.

| rules | construction | k-means | FCM | construction, before the fix |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 2 | 6.6 ms | 10.4 | **4.1** | 25.7 |
| 3 | 8.7 | 10.4 | **6.9** | 34.1 |
| 4 | **10.8** | 12.7 | 20.7 | 45.5 |
| 6 | 14.9 | **13.7** | 30.0 | 62.3 |
| 8 | **19.4** | 20.2 | 34.8 | 82.9 |
| 12 | 30.5 | **28.0** | 72.4 | 118.3 |

This study opened by reporting classical identification as **23–84× cheaper**
than the construction. Addendum 3 cut that to 1.9–6.6× by matching parameter
counts. With the library fixed it is **0.5–1.6× — parity**, with the winner
changing from row to row.

The 23–84× was never a property of the method. It was three things stacked: an
unmatched parameter count, a model-selection search the classical route is never
asked to perform, and a `KMeans(n_clusters=1)` call for a partition that is the
whole dataset.

## PhiUSIIL, like for like

Same 20,000-row cap on both columns, so this isolates the selector:

| train rows | before | after | speedup | accuracy, before | after |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 4,000 | 407 ms | **63** | 6.5× | 0.9997 | 0.9997 |
| 40,000 | 1,776 | **222** | 8.0× | 0.9998 | 0.9998 |
| 188,636 | 2,042 | **374** | 5.5× | 0.9997 | 0.9998 |

Choosing the component count was **82%** of full-size training. It is now 24%.

## PhiUSIIL as it now runs — no cap on anything

Every route reads every row. This is the comparison Addendum 3 could not make.

| train rows | construction | k-means | FCM |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 4,000 | 60 ms | 15 | 47 |
| 40,000 | 197 | 117 | 460 |
| 96,000 | 434 | 252 | 932 |
| 188,636 | **725** | 496 | 2,752 |

Pinned to a matched parameter count, at 188,636 rows: construction **582 ms**
against k-means **492 ms** and FCM **3,111 ms**. Accuracy, all three seeds:

| route | test accuracy | errors per 10,000 | membership fns |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| construction | **0.9998 ± 0.0000** | **2** | 43 |

**1.2× the cost of k-means for 25× fewer errors, with 43 membership functions
against 60.** The construction's cost curve is now honestly linear — it reads
every row, and it says so.

## What the thesis can now claim

Addendum 3's summary was *"classical wins small, construction wins large,
crossover observed"*, with the crossover explained away by a subsampling cap.
That is superseded:

> At matched parameter counts and with every route reading every row, the
> Gaussian construction identifies a rule base at **the same cost as classical
> clustering** — parity on Concrete, within 1.2× of k-means and 4× cheaper than
> fuzzy c-means on PhiUSIIL — and on the larger, higher-dimensional problem it
