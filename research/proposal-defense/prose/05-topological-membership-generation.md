# Chapter 5 — Membership Functions from Topological Structure (Proposed)

*This is proposed work. The construction and the preliminary results below are real and reproducible today; the extensions in Section 5.5 are what I propose to complete for the dissertation.*

## 5.1 Introduction

Chapter 4 builds a fuzzy model fast, but it makes two assumptions that are not always available. It assumes the data has coordinates, so that a Gaussian can be fit to it, and it assumes the structure is roughly blob-shaped, so that a Gaussian is the right thing to fit. Plenty of real data satisfies neither. Sequences compared by dynamic time warping, strings compared by edit distance, and graphs compared by a kernel come to me as a dissimilarity matrix and nothing else — no coordinates to average, no natural notion of a Gaussian. And plenty of structure is not blob-shaped: two concentric rings are a textbook case where every centroid method fails, because the mean of a ring sits in the middle of the hole.

The tool I already trust for this setting is VAT, from Chapter 3, which works on exactly a dissimilarity matrix and finds structure of any shape because it is built on single-linkage connectivity rather than on centroids. But VAT, and the whole VAT family, stops at *assessment* — it tells me how many clusters there are and roughly where, and then hands off. No one in that literature turns the VAT structure into a fuzzy model. That gap is what this chapter is about: taking the minimax hierarchy that VAT and iVAT produce and reading fuzzy membership functions directly off it — the number of clusters, the number of *scales*, and the membership functions themselves — with no coordinates and no Gaussian assumption. This is the bridge between the clustering half of the dissertation and the fuzzy-modeling half, and it is the piece I consider the most novel.

The contributions I propose are: the minimax transform as the preprocessing step that makes relational fuzzy clustering succeed on non-convex data; a selection rule that treats the number of clusters as an *output* rather than an input, by gating persistence and covering the data; a multi-scale extension that recovers a whole hierarchy of partitions and discovers how many scales are present; and membership functions extracted natively from each piece of the hierarchy, ready to serve as the antecedents of a fuzzy inference system.

## 5.2 Background and Prior Art

I have to be careful and honest here, because the nearest prior work is close. Persistence-based clustering already exists: ToMATo [Chazal et al. 2013] and, most pointedly, the beta-plateau method of Bonis and Oudot (2014, 2018) already derive a cluster count from gaps in a persistence diagram, and there is recent automated work in the same vein [AuToMATo]. I am not going to claim persistence-for-clustering as new. What I claim is different on three specific axes, and I state them plainly so the distinction survives scrutiny. First, my filtration is the *connectivity* (single-linkage, ultrametric) structure, which is density-free, rather than a density mode-seeking filtration. Second, my membership function is a *deterministic* ramp read off the merge heights, not a random-walk hitting probability. Third, and most importantly, my target is not a clustering — it is a set of *membership functions for a fuzzy inference system*: linguistic antecedents, combined by disjunction, that a downstream FIS consumes. Nobody in the persistence literature, and nobody in the VAT family, is producing that object. If a reviewer collapses the first two axes, the third still stands.

The other prior art I lean on is relational: NERFCM [Hathaway and Bezdek 1994], which runs Fuzzy C-Means directly on a dissimilarity matrix, with a beta-spread safeguard for when the matrix is badly non-metric; and ConiVAT [Rathore et al. 2020], which repairs single-linkage's chaining failure with constraint-based metric learning.

### The concrete version of this problem, in my own code

Before the method, the failure it repairs — because it is not hypothetical, and
it is sitting in my own library rather than in someone else's.

`tribble-cluster` ships `IVATMeans`, a scikit-learn-style wrapper that uses iVAT
to find clusters and then hands them to a Euclidean back end. Those two halves
live in incompatible geometries, and the seam is exactly where the method fails.

The iVAT recurrence $D'[r,c] = \max(D^*[r,j], D'[j,c])$ computes the minimax path
distance — equivalently the single-linkage distance, the largest edge on the MST
path between two points. Minimax distances form an **ultrametric**, and that is
precisely why VAT and iVAT handle elongated, chained and non-convex structure
well: they never assume a cluster is a blob.

Having recovered such a cluster, `IVATMeans` then represents it by
`np.mean(points, axis=0)` and refines with Euclidean fuzzy $c$-means. A mean is a
Euclidean prototype, and $c$-means assigns by Euclidean distance, so both are
constrained to convex regions. The mean of a ring is at its centre, where there
are no points; the mean of a filament is off the filament. On the very data where
iVAT beats $k$-means, the back end discards the advantage the front end just
earned — it re-merges the two moons that iVAT had cut perfectly.

I state it plainly because it sharpens what this chapter is for. The fix is not a
better centroid heuristic; there is no good Euclidean prototype for a ring. The
fix is to stay in the minimax geometry all the way through — transform the
dissimilarities once, then use a method that consumes a dissimilarity matrix
rather than coordinates, which is what relational FCM does and what §5.4 measures.
Chapter 3's contribution is making that transform affordable at scale; this
chapter's is showing what to do with it once you have it.

It also locates the work relative to the literature more precisely than a claim of
novelty usually can. The VAT lineage has stayed on the crisp, visual side —
clusiVAT, aVAT, SpecVAT, ML-aVAT, kernel-iVAT — while the fuzzy relational lineage
never adopted the VAT/minimax ordering. The gap between them is not a gap in
anyone's argument; it is simply unoccupied, and `IVATMeans` is what falls into it
when you build the obvious thing without noticing the geometries disagree.

## 5.3 Methodology

### 5.3.1 The minimax transform does the heavy lifting

I want to be honest about where the result actually comes from, because it would be easy to over-credit the clever part. The single most important step is the minimax (iVAT) transform from Chapter 2 — replacing each dissimilarity with the bottleneck value along the best path between two points. On concentric rings, ordinary relational Fuzzy C-Means run on the raw dissimilarity matrix scores an adjusted Rand index of about 0.02, which is to say it fails completely. Run the *same* algorithm on the minimax-transformed matrix and it scores 1.00. The transform, not the selection machinery I describe next, is what carries that result, and I say so up front. What the rest of this chapter adds is how to choose the clusters and turn them into membership functions without being told how many there are.

**Figure 5.1 — Why the transform is the load-bearing step.** Concentric rings, shown as the raw dissimilarity matrix $D$ and as the minimax-transformed $D^*$, with the relational-FCM ARI from Table 5.1 annotated on each. Same points, same ordering, same algorithm; only the dissimilarity changes, and the score moves from 0.02 to 1.00. One thing the picture is more precise about than the sentence above it: under $D$ the *inner* ring does read as a block. It is the outer ring that does not, because its own within-ring distances reach 8.4 while the nearest inner-to-outer distance is 2.6 — which is exactly the configuration no Euclidean prototype can resolve, and exactly what the bottleneck ultrametric fixes.
`![minimax-transform](fig/05-minimax-transform.png)`

### 5.3.2 Selection as gated set-cover, with k as an output

Given the minimax hierarchy, the usual question is "where do I cut it?" — which presupposes I know how many clusters to look for. I reframe it.

A word on terminology first. By a **block** I mean a candidate cluster in the hierarchy: a set of points that merges into one connected group at some threshold and later merges into something larger. It is a node of the single-linkage dendrogram, and it is also, not coincidentally, one of the dark squares along the diagonal of the reordered VAT image from Chapter 2 — which is where the name comes from. Every block has a birth height (where it forms) and a death height (where it is absorbed), and the hierarchy contains many more blocks than there are real clusters; the job of selection is to decide which ones to believe.

Each block has a persistence — its death height minus its birth height, from Chapter 2 — and I admit only the blocks whose persistence is a statistical outlier, above the median by a robust multiple of the median absolute deviation. Among the admitted blocks I then greedily cover the data, taking at each step the block that covers the most still-uncovered points. The number of clusters falls out of this process; it is an output, not an input. The reason to frame it as coverage rather than as "pick exactly k" is asymmetry: under a fixed disjunction, admitting one too many blocks is cheap, while failing to cover part of the data is expensive, so a set-cover is the right shape for the objective.

I should say now, rather than let it surface later as a surprise, that this gate is not uniformly the best selector available. It is deliberately conservative: it declines to assert structure it cannot see clearly, which is the behavior I want on noise and the wrong behavior on a chained "bridge" between two real clusters, where a more aggressive selector wins outright. §5.4 reports that comparison in full, including the case my gate loses. The conservatism is a defensible choice for this pipeline — a fuzzy model built on spurious antecedents is worse than one that abstains — but it is a choice, not a free lunch. One qualification I owe up front, because it narrows the trade-off rather than the method: the conservatism described here is a property of the *flat* gate. The multi-scale selector of §5.3.3, run on the same bridged data, does not pay it — §5.4 reports both runs and which one the tables quote.

### 5.3.3 Multi-scale persistence: recovering the whole hierarchy

The most interesting case, and the one I want to be the headline of this chapter, is nested structure — clusters within clusters, where each level is real. A single flat cut cannot represent that; it stops at whichever granularity covers the data first. The idea that unlocks it is the density observation from Chapter 2: birth height in single-linkage is an inverse proxy for local density, so structures at different densities are born at different heights. I look for gaps along the log-birth axis, use them to separate the hierarchy into density *bands*, and run the same gated set-cover *within each band*. The result is not a single partition but a stack of them — a fuzzy hierarchy — and the number of scales is itself discovered rather than specified.

**Figure 5.2 — Band discovery on the three-level hierarchy: the mechanism and its result in one figure.** This is the chapter's key figure, and it is the real selector — `select_multiscale` on the same dataset and through the same call as Table 5.2's `three_level_hierarchy` row, so the band edges drawn are the edges the algorithm found. Left: every persistence-significant block placed at its birth height, on a log axis, with the two gaps that separate the bands marked and the bands shaded. Right: the partition each band recovers, at granularities 8, 4 and 2, with the samples ordered by the finest ground-truth label so that a recovered cluster is a contiguous run of colour and the nesting is visible by eye. Each level scores ARI 1.00 against its own truth. The partitions are strips rather than scatters for a mundane reason worth stating: the construction offsets its levels by 200, 28 and 5 in data units at $\sigma = 0.35$ — that is 571$\sigma$, 80$\sigma$ and 14$\sigma$ — so in a scatter the fine structure this figure exists to show is smaller than the marker. Those separations are also the reason the mechanism is legible here at all, which the Table 5.2 caption takes up.
`![band-discovery](fig/05-band-discovery.png)`

I want to frame this honestly, because there is a strawman available that I refuse to set up. It would be tempting to claim that this beats a flat method on varying-density data, and I do not claim it: a flat cover appears to hold up across a wide range of cluster widths, though the experiment that was supposed to establish that is inconclusive as run (see the falsification result below, which I now report as inconclusive rather than as a refutation). The genuine open problem is not varying density at a single level; it is *nested* structure across levels, which a flat method structurally cannot recover. The multi-scale method is close to a strict generalization, and I have to state the "close to" precisely rather than claim the clean version. On the five single-scale battery datasets, band discovery finds **exactly one band on four of them** and **no bands at all on pure noise** (`multiscale_no_regression.ms_n_bands`), which is the behaviour the generalization claim needs. Within that single band the cover then matches the flat selector exactly on three — two_gaussians, concentric_rings, varying_density, same $k$ and same ARI to three decimals — and *differs* on bridged_gaussians, where it returns $k$ = 2 at ARI 1.000 against the flat selector's $k$ = 3 at 0.982. So it is a near-reduction rather than an identity, and the two datasets where it departs from the flat selector are the two where the flat selector was wrong: it finds no structure in noise where the flat gate reports four blocks, and it finds the right count on the bridge where the flat gate does not. §5.4 reports that disagreement in full rather than folding it into the flat numbers.

### 5.3.4 Membership functions, read off the hierarchy

Each selected block carries its own membership function, with no medoid and no Gaussian fit. The natural one is a persistence ramp. Let $d_B(x)$ be the minimax distance from the point $x$ to the block $B$ — that is, the bottleneck height at which $x$ would join $B$ in the hierarchy, which is the same quantity the transform of §5.3.1 already computes. Membership then falls linearly from one, for a point that joins as early as the block itself was born, to zero, for a point that only joins when the block dies:

$$ \mu_B(x) = \mathrm{clip}\!\left(\frac{\text{death}_B - d_B(x)}{\text{death}_B - \text{birth}_B},\, 0,\, 1\right). $$

Every term is a merge height the hierarchy already gives me, so the membership function is *read off* the structure rather than fitted to it. That is the property that makes this work without coordinates: there is no mean to compute and no shape to assume. The denominator is exactly the block's persistence, which also means the gate of §5.3.2 and the membership function are two uses of the same quantity — a long-persistence block is both more likely to be admitted and gentler in its membership falloff.

There is a catch in the ramp, and I would rather state it than let a reader plot it and find it. **On an ultrametric the ramp is crisp.** For a member of $B$, $d_B(x)$ is at most $\text{birth}_B$; for a non-member it is at least $\text{death}_B$, because $\text{death}_B$ is by definition the height at which $B$ first absorbs anything outside itself. Nothing can land strictly between the two, so the sloped part of the ramp is empty by construction and $\mu_B$ takes only the values one and zero. This is a property of the geometry, not a defect of the implementation, and the shipped code already accounts for it: the default kernel in `multiscale_persistence.block_membership` is a Gaussian in minimax distance with its half-maximum at the block's death height, which grades the non-member skirt and is genuinely fuzzy, while `argmax` over it still reproduces the crisp labels. The ramp is kept alongside it, and the claim that matters — that every parameter is a merge height rather than a fitted quantity — is equally true of both. Figure 5.3 shows the two together with the data on the axis, so the empty interval is visible rather than asserted.

**Figure 5.3 — The membership function, read off the hierarchy.** One selected block's $\mu_B(x)$ against minimax distance, annotated with the block's birth and death heights, next to the fuzzy partition the whole band produces. Both curves are drawn: the persistence ramp above, and the kernel the code actually ships. Every sample is plotted at its own $d_B$ along the axis, which is what makes the previous paragraph's point visible — the interval the ramp slopes across contains no data, so the ramp is a step and the Gaussian is the one doing the grading. Neither is fitted: both are parameterized entirely by merge heights the dendrogram already supplies, which is the claim this figure exists to support.
`![persistence-ramp](fig/05-persistence-ramp.png)`

I have several variants of this — a Ruspini partition-of-unity form where the memberships sum to one exactly, a spread-aware auto-tuned form, and an interpretable feature-space form that works when coordinates *are* available (and, honestly, fails on rings, which is the point of having the others). The blocks are combined into a rule by disjunction, a t-conorm, exactly as the OR of antecedents in Chapter 4. The upshot is that the same minimax hierarchy that told me how many clusters there are also hands me the linguistic antecedents of a fuzzy model, directly.

### 5.3.5 Counting disjuncts topologically

There is one more consequence worth drawing out, because it answers a question Chapter 4 left open. The rules in Chapter 4 are disjunctions — a class is recognized if *this* Gaussian fires or *that* one does — and the number of terms in that OR is a parameter I had to choose. Here it is determined by the data by construction. A class occupying two separated regions of the input space appears in the minimax structure as two connected components at the relevant threshold, so the arity of the disjunction is a component count rather than a hyperparameter.

I have to mark that as a construction-level claim and not a measured one, because no recorded run has exercised it. `results.json` → `arity_detection` reports arity 1 for every ground-truth block on every battery dataset, under both modes the driver runs (`dstar` and `geometric`) — including both 80-point blocks of the concentric rings. The counter has therefore never returned a value other than one, so nothing in the results establishes that it discriminates a two-piece class from a one-piece class; it establishes only that it does not hallucinate extra disjuncts. Exercising it needs a dataset with a genuinely disconnected class, which the battery does not contain.

The reason this matters is that the same question is ill-posed geometrically. Asking how many convex pieces a ring consists of has no good answer — a ring is one connected object that no finite set of blobs covers naturally, which is exactly why the feature-space variant of §5.3.4 fails on rings. Asking how many connected components it has is well-posed and returns one. So the topological formulation both answers the question and explains why the geometric one could not.

*Caption, continued — the simplest baseline, which the results file has and the table above does not.* `results.json` → `main_table.kmeans_ari` records plain $k$-means given $k$ on the same data: **1.00** on well_separated, **1.00** on bridged_gaussians, **0.98** on varying_density, and **0.017** on concentric_rings (null on noise, which has no partition). **On the discovery, including where it is wrong.** The gate discovers $k$ correctly on **three of the four datasets that have a ground-truth $k$**: 2 on the rings, 2 on well_separated, 3 on varying_density. On bridged_gaussians it returns **$k$ = 3 where the truth is 2, at 53.3% coverage** — and, as Table 5.3 shows, beta-plateau and bottleneck-bootstrap both return the correct 2 there, at 98.3% and 97.5% coverage. So the framing I would like to use — "the baselines are handed $k$ and mine finds it" — is fair against NERFCM, ConiVAT and $k$-means, and is not fair against the two persistence selectors, which also discover $k$ and get it right on all four labeled datasets where I get three. That is a real loss, and the wrong count and the low coverage are two faces of one thing rather than two separate faults. `selector_comparison.bridged_gaussians.coverage_cover` records the actual cover: three blocks of sizes **58, 3, 3**, purity 1.000, coverage 0.533. Every block it admitted is pure; it simply admitted one half of the data plus two three-point fragments and never admitted the other half at all. The count is wrong *because* the second real cluster is missing, not because a spurious large cluster was invented.

The uniform-noise row is where declining is the right answer, which no accuracy-style metric rewards, and I have to be exact about what the gate does there rather than call it abstention. `N/A` is not an omission — uniform noise has no ground-truth partition, so an adjusted Rand index is undefined against it for every method — but the gate does not report nothing either. `main_table.uniform_noise` records `cover_nblocks` = **4** and `cover_coverage` = **0.125**: four blocks, claiming an eighth of the points. The near-abstention is in the coverage, not in the block count.

One column deserves a word on how it is scored, because the bridged row looks contradictory without it. The set-cover on bridged Gaussians reaches ARI 0.982 *on the points it claims*, but claims only 53.3% of them; scored over the whole dataset, with everything uncovered counted as unassigned, it is 0.001. Both numbers describe the same cover, and both are in the JSON — `main_table.cover_ari` and `persistence_methods.bridged_gaussians.methods.persistence_gap.ari`. The table now carries both, and the prose quotes the all-points figure, which is the conservative reading and the one that makes the row a genuine failure rather than a partial success.

**The bridge case, reconciled — because two runs in the same results file disagree about it.** I am reporting the bridge as a failure, and I have to disclose that a different run in the same JSON solves it. The failure is the *flat* gated cover: $k$ = 3, coverage 0.533, all-points ARI 0.001. But `multiscale_no_regression.bridged_gaussians` runs the **multi-scale selector** on that same dataset and records `ms_n_bands` = 1, `ms_finest_k` = **2** — the correct count — and `ms_finest_ari` = **1.000**. The comparison is apples-to-apples on scoring: both that row's `flat_ari` (0.982, at `flat_k` = 3) and its `ms_finest_ari` use the same nearest-block assignment, `multiscale_persistence.assign`, which assigns every point to the nearest block rather than leaving uncovered points out. So under one consistent scoring the flat gate gets 0.982 with the wrong $k$ and the multi-scale selector gets 1.000 with the right one.

I am not quietly upgrading the row, for two reasons. First, Tables 5.1 and 5.3 report the flat gate throughout, because the bake-off's competitors — beta-plateau and bottleneck-bootstrap — are flat selectors, and swapping in my multi-scale result while leaving theirs flat would be exactly the kind of asymmetric comparison this section exists to avoid. Second, `multiscale_no_regression` records no coverage for the multi-scale band, so I cannot state its all-points score under the conservative scoring the tables use, and I will not quote a number I do not have. What this does to §5.3.2's trade-off is narrow it: the noise-conservatism-versus-bridge-robustness tension is a property of the flat gate, not of the pipeline the chapter actually proposes as its headline. Establishing that properly means running the multi-scale selector through the same bake-off — same datasets, same scoring, coverage recorded — which is a cheap addition to `run_persistence_methods_numeric()` and belongs with the prior-art head-to-head in §5.5.

**Multi-scale, the headline — and the aggregation that inflates it.** The claim I want to make here is structural, not an accuracy gain, and the earlier version of this paragraph made the wrong one. It read: "averaged over all ground-truth levels, the multi-scale method lifts nested Gaussians from 0.66 to 1.00, a three-level hierarchy from 0.58 to 1.00, and a density hierarchy from 0.75 to 1.00." Those means are correct — `multiscale_hierarchy.*.flat_mean_ari` is 0.662, 0.576 and 0.746, and `ms_mean_ari` is 1.000 in all three — but they compare unlike things. The flat column is **one partition scored against every level and averaged**; the multi-scale column is a **per-level maximum over the bands** (`ms_best_ari_per_level`, computed as `max` over band assignments for each level) and then averaged. A single flat partition cannot beat roughly $1/L$ on an $L$-level average however good it is, because $L-1$ of its scores are scored against granularities it does not have. Most of the 0.576 → 1.000 gap is that asymmetry, not the method.

The harness's own caption says as much, and I would rather quote it than paraphrase: *"Averaging over ALL ground-truth levels is what makes the flat column look bad — a flat cover lands one level exactly and misses the rest, so its per-level scores are recorded in `flat_ari_per_level`."*

So here is the defensible version. **A flat cover returns one partition; the band stack returns $L$, one per true level.** On all three datasets the flat cover returns $k$ = 2 and scores exactly **1.000 on one true level** — the coarsest — and much less on the rest. The band stack returns as many partitions as there are levels, and each band's own score vector peaks at a distinct level, one-to-one and in order: on the three-level set, band 0 ($k$ = 8) scores [1.000, 0.581, 0.236] across fine/medium/coarse, band 1 ($k$ = 4) scores [0.581, 1.000, 0.492], and band 2 ($k$ = 2) scores [0.236, 0.492, 1.000]. That the maximum is attained by a *different* band for each level, in monotone order, is what makes the per-level max something other than an oracle trick — but it is still a max, and the **0.58 → 1.00 pair must not be read as an accuracy gain**. What was measured is that the number of partitions returned went from one to three, each correct at its own granularity, without the method being told there were three levels.

|---|:--|---:|:--|---:|:--|---:|:--:|

*Caption — how easy this data makes the problem, stated with the numbers.* Band discovery has exactly one stated assumption: that the density scales are well separated. The dataset that demonstrates it satisfies that assumption by one to three orders of magnitude. `battery_hierarchical.three_level_hierarchy` uses `sigma` = 0.35 with `l3_sep` = 5.0, `l2_sep` = 28.0 and `l1_sep` = 200.0 — separations of **14σ, 80σ and 571σ**, with consecutive scales differing by factors of **5.6×** and **7.1×**. The discovered band edges sit at birth heights **1.593** and **10.311**, which is the gap those factors produce. It is also small and singly-realized: `n_per` = 12 over 8 leaves gives **n = 96** points, at a **fixed `seed` = 11**, with no spread reported over seeds anywhere in `multiscale_hierarchy`. I therefore read Table 5.2 as establishing that the **mechanism works** — gaps on the log-birth axis do separate genuine scale levels, and the gated cover within each band does recover that level exactly — and not as establishing that it is robust. Where it stops working is not measured, and §5.5 proposes the experiment that would measure it.

**The falsification experiment — which I now report as inconclusive, not as a refutation.** The intent was to keep myself honest: show that a flat cover already handles single-level varying density, so that the multi-scale method is not claiming credit for a problem that was never open. `multiscale_scale_invariance` sweeps six contrast settings, and the first thing to fix is the range. The sweep runs contrast 1.5 → 8.0, which is `spread_ratio` **1:1.5:2.25 up to 1:8:64** — a **64-fold** spread between the narrowest and widest cluster at the top setting, not the thirty-fold I wrote. At every one of the six settings the flat cover returns `flat_k` = **3** and `flat_ari` = **0.983**.

An identical score to three decimals at all six settings is either a very clean invariance or a sign that the swept parameter did not change what is being scored, and I cannot tell which from the recorded output, because **nothing recorded moves**. The JSON stores three fields per setting — `spread_ratio`, `flat_k`, `flat_ari` — and two of the three are constant across the sweep. Worse, the generator scales the inter-cluster separation *with* the spread (`xs[-1] + sep * (sig[j-1] + sig[j]) / 2` at `sep` = 6.0), so every cluster's separation-to-spread ratio is held fixed by construction and each configuration is close to a rescaling of the last — which is a good reason for a scale-relative selector not to move, and equally a reason the experiment cannot distinguish invariance from a no-op.

This repository holds itself to that standard elsewhere: `PROVENANCE_MAP.md` note 12 downgrades a result to inconclusive because its manipulation check failed, and this one has no manipulation check at all. So the honest status is **inconclusive**, and the fix is cheap and specific: record, per contrast setting, a quantity that *should* move with contrast — the number of bands `select_multiscale` finds, and the range of block birth heights on the log axis — and show whether it does. If those move while `flat_ari` holds, the invariance is real and the claim stands; if they do not move either, the sweep is not varying what it purports to vary and the experiment has to be redesigned. Until then §5.3.3's "flat methods already handle single-level varying density" rests on the flat cover's 0.983 at one setting rather than on a demonstrated invariance across six.

**Scaling — pending, with no output artifact.** This paragraph previously read: "With the exact $O(N^2)$ minimax transform, the full pipeline runs to 5,000 points in about five seconds, and the multi-scale recovery of [8, 4, 2] is unchanged from 100 points up to 5,000." I cannot support either half from anything on disk, so I am marking it pending rather than quoting it. `results.json` has **no `scaling` key**. The benchmark is opt-in: `run_all.py --scaling` runs *only* `run_scaling_benchmark()` (sizes 100, 250, 500, 1000, 2000, 5000, using `ivat_mf.minimax_transform_fast`, the exact $O(n^2)$ union-find bottleneck fill) and writes a **separate** `outputs/scaling_results.json` plus `outputs/fig11_scaling.png`, explicitly without touching `results.json` — and neither of those two files exists. `PROVENANCE_MAP.md` corroborates it from the other side: the driver "regenerates 16 of its 17 figures (`fig11_scaling` is behind an opt-in `--scaling` flag)".

The invariance claim is doubly unsupported. The benchmark's nested family is `battery_hierarchical.scalable_many_scale` (`l1_sep` 400, `l2_sep` 55, `l3_sep` 8, σ 0.6) — a *different* generator from Table 5.2's `three_level_hierarchy`, whose only recorded run is the single n = 96 realization above. So there is no recorded run of the [8, 4, 2] recovery at any sample size other than 96, and the "unchanged from 100 up to 5,000" sentence describes a table that has never been written. Producing it is one command, run from inside the module directory because `OUT` is the relative `./outputs` — `cd gated-minimax-selection && python run_all.py --scaling` — and until it is run and registered in `reproduce/manifest.py`, no timing or invariance number belongs in this chapter.

**The selection bake-off.** Comparing my persistence-gap gate against beta-plateau and bottleneck-bootstrap, there is no universal winner, and I report that as a finding rather than hide it. My gate fails a deliberately adversarial "bridge" case (ARI 0.001, $k$ = 3 against a true 2, coverage 0.533) but claims only an eighth of the noise; beta-plateau and bottleneck-bootstrap fix the bridge (0.927 and 0.891, both at the correct $k$ = 2, at 98.3% and 97.5% coverage) but are less conservative on noise, both reporting seven clusters where there are none.

That last comparison needs the coverage column to be read honestly, and adding it costs me part of my own claim. Abstention here is not a matter of reporting no clusters — my gate *does* report four on uniform noise — it is a matter of how much of the data a method is willing to commit to. Mine claims 12.5% of the noise points. Beta-plateau claims 95.8%, which is genuine over-firing. But bottleneck-bootstrap claims only 25%, far closer to my gate than to beta-plateau, while also repairing the bridge. So the trade-off between bridge-robustness and noise-conservatism is real but it is a *gradient*, not the dichotomy I first described, and bottleneck-bootstrap is the serious competitor: it does both things at once, less cleanly than I do one and than beta-plateau does the other. I would rather state that than let the coarser reading stand.

The two restored columns cost me the cleanest version of my framing, which is why they have to be here. On the bridge my gate is not merely less accurate — it gets the *count* wrong (3 against 2) while both competitors get it right, and it does so while committing to half the data. Across the four labeled battery datasets the two competitors discover $k$ correctly four times out of four; my gate manages three. The one axis where I am unambiguously ahead is noise coverage, and even there bottleneck-bootstrap is much closer to me than to beta-plateau.

**Relational-only data — the one experiment with no coordinates, and it does not measure my selector.** On the three dissimilarity-matrix-only datasets built from tree and graph shortest paths, the simple cases are already solved by NERFCM (`three_clusters_tree`, n = 30, ARI 1.000 on both $D$ and $D^*$; `chain_then_ring`, n = 40, 1.000 on both), while a genuinely multi-scale relational case leaves both the raw and transformed matrices stuck at ARI **0.285** (`multi_scale_hierarchy`, n = 39) — confirming that multi-scale relational structure is the honestly hard, still-open problem. The measurement to keep in view, and the reason for the limit stated before Table 5.1, is that all six of those numbers are NERFCM. This is the only genuinely coordinate-free data in the chapter and the gated set-cover was never run on it.

**What these results do not yet show.** Every number above is a *clustering* score, and the chapter's stated purpose is not clustering — it is generating membership functions for a fuzzy inference system. So the result that would most directly support the claim is missing: I have not yet built a FIS from these membership functions and measured how it predicts. Adjusted Rand index against a known partition is a reasonable proxy, since antecedents that carve up the space correctly are the precondition for a good model, but it is a proxy and I should not let it stand in for the real thing. Closing that gap — wiring the generated membership functions into the Chapter 6 inference system and reporting end-to-end accuracy against the Chapter 4 Gaussian construction on the same data — is one of the two deliverables §5.5 puts first, alongside running the selector on data that never had coordinates. Together they are what would turn this chapter from a promising method into a demonstrated one.

## 5.5 Proposed Work

What turns this from a strong preliminary result into a dissertation chapter is the following. The goal labels refer to Chapter 7, where these are scheduled.

First, **real non-coordinate data (G2)** — which I have moved to the front of this list because it is the measurement the chapter's central claim does not have. Everything scored above is a coordinate-generated point set with a Euclidean dissimilarity matrix; the only coordinate-free data in the chapter is measured with NERFCM, not with my selector. The claim that this works where there are no coordinates is therefore a property of the construction and nothing more, and it stays that way until the gated set-cover and the multi-scale selector are run on matrices that never had coordinates behind them.

That splits into a cheap step and a real one, and I want the cheap one on the record because there is no excuse for it being missing. The cheap step needs no new data: run `select_coverage_cover` and `select_multiscale` on the three `relationdata.py` matrices already in the harness, and record discovered $k$, coverage and per-level ARI beside the existing NERFCM columns. That alone converts the central claim from a code-reading argument into a table. The real step is genuine non-metric domains — time series under dynamic time warping, sequences under edit distance, graphs under a kernel — which is the same gap I named in Chapter 3 and a shared goal for completion. It is also the setting where the honestly hard case above (multi-scale relational structure at ARI 0.285) has to be confronted rather than reported.

Second, and equally load-bearing, the **end-to-end integration** — the deliverable the previous section named as the missing evidence. The whole point is to feed a fuzzy model, so the closing result must be a FIS built from these membership functions, evaluated end to end from a bare dissimilarity matrix to a working, readable rule base, and compared against the Chapter 4 Gaussian construction on data where both can run. Until that exists, the chapter's other central claim rests on a clustering proxy. This feeds the capstone of Chapter 7.

Third, **direct, one-pass membership generation (G1).** Right now the pipeline selects clusters and then fits membership functions in two stages; I propose to collapse that into a single pass, in which every block emits its native ramp membership, the disjunction recombines them, and the surviving envelope simply *is* the fuzzy model. The research-interesting piece within that is a soft, kernel-weighted band membership, which I expect to fix the over-segmentation that shows up at small sample sizes.

Fourth, the **prior-art head-to-head.** I owe a direct comparison against Bonis–Oudot beta-plateau and AuToMATo on identical data, and a formal literature search to bound the novelty claim. Given how close that prior work is, this is a defensive necessity as much as a scientific one — and Table 5.3, where both of them discover $k$ correctly four times out of four against my three, is the reason it cannot wait. The same run should carry the multi-scale selector through the bake-off on the same footing, which is what §5.4's bridge reconciliation is missing.

Fifth, **joint memberships where the data demands them (G8, stretch).** Every membership function in this dissertation is one-dimensional: Chapter 4 fits an independent Gaussian per feature and combines them with a t-conorm, and the ramps of §5.3.4 are read off a single axis at a time. That factorization is what keeps the rule count linear rather than exponential, and it is most of why the models are fast. It is also a hard expressive limit, and the ring makes it visible. A ring is not the intersection of per-axis intervals, so *no* collection of 1-D memberships describes one, at any resolution. This is the same wall §5.2's `IVATMeans` hits, reached from the membership side rather than the prototype side: there is no good axis-aligned description of a ring for the same reason there is no good Euclidean centroid for one.

The obvious response is a membership defined jointly over two features rather than per-feature. I want to propose it carefully, because it spends the thing this dissertation is selling. "Age is high AND cement is high" is a clause a person reads; a joint membership over an annulus in (cement, water) space is a picture. Worse, Chapter 6 commits explicitly to Magdalena's condition — every gate and split over an original, named input, never a synthetic intermediate — and an unrestricted joint membership is close to the construction that condition exists to forbid.

So the proposal is not "use 2-D memberships." It is **use them only where the structure requires it, and let the data decide where that is.** A cluster whose one-dimensional projections already separate it keeps its 1-D memberships and stays readable; a cluster that exists only jointly — the ring — gets a 2-D membership, and interpretability is spent on exactly the pieces that cannot be had otherwise.

An earlier version of this paragraph said the detector for that decision "is already built: §5.3.5 counts a class's connected components topologically." That is wrong twice over and I am cutting it. It is wrong on availability — the component counter reports arity 1 for every block on every dataset in `arity_detection`, under both modes the driver runs, so it has never returned any other value. And it is wrong in principle, which §5.3.5's own text concedes: a ring *is* one connected component. Component counting cannot distinguish a ring from a blob, and that is precisely the discrimination this goal needs.

The results file does contain a detector that separates the cases, and it is not the one I claimed. `results.json` → `feature_space` scores each dataset's blocks twice — once with memberships in dissimilarity space, once with a feature-space surrogate fitted to each block's own coordinates — and records the gap. On **concentric rings** the gap is total: `ari_dissimilarity` **1.0000** against `ari_feature_space` **0.0000**, `ari_gap` **1.0000**. On **bridged Gaussians** it is large: **0.8352** against **0.0864**, gap **0.7488**. On **varying density**, where a coordinate description is perfectly adequate, there is **no gap at all** — 0.9799 against 0.9799, gap **0.0000** — and the same on well_separated (1.0000 / 1.0000 / 0.0000). That is exactly the shape a detector needs: large where the structure is only relational, zero where coordinates suffice. Two honest qualifications. The surrogate is a full-covariance Mahalanobis Gaussian per block (`feature_space_mf.evaluate_feature_space_membership`), not the per-axis product Chapter 4 actually uses — which makes it *more* expressive than an axis-aligned family, so its failure is a conservative bound on what per-axis memberships can do, and its success does not by itself prove an axis-aligned form would succeed. And the surrogate's L2 fidelity is *not* the discriminating statistic: `surrogate_l2_mean` is 0.2915 on rings but 0.1041 on varying density against 0.0777 on the bridge, so it would rank varying density above the bridge. The ARI gap separates the cases; the L2 error does not.

Building that into a decision rule — thresholding the per-block ARI gap, or the per-block equivalent of it, and promoting only the blocks above threshold to a joint membership — is **proposed work, not existing machinery.** What exists is the diagnostic that shows the signal is there. That is enough to make the decision a measured property of the data rather than a modelling preference, which is the standard I have tried to hold everywhere else in this chapter, but it is not enough to call the detector built.

Two things would have to be shown for this to be a contribution rather than an escape hatch. That the joint memberships are *rare* on real data — if most clusters need one, the interpretability claim collapses and the honest conclusion is that this construction is the wrong tool for that data. And that a rule base mixing 1-D and 2-D antecedents is still readable end to end, which is a question for a person to answer, not a metric. I mark this a stretch goal for that reason: the joint memberships themselves are a modest extension of what exists, the detector that decides where to use them has to be built, and the case that either is worth having is empirical and not yet made.

Sixth, **band discovery for overlapping scales (G7, stretch)** — which I am splitting in two, because Table 5.2's caption makes its assumption the chapter's largest unmeasured one. The gap heuristic on the log-birth axis assumes the density scales are well separated, and the dataset that demonstrates it separates them by 14σ, 80σ and 571σ, with consecutive scales a factor of 5.6× and 7.1× apart. Nothing measures what happens closer in. The *fix* — a model-based discovery, a change-point or barcode-stability criterion — stays a stretch goal and is still the piece most likely to be cut if time runs short. The *measurement* should not be a stretch goal, because it is cheap and it is what a reader will actually ask for.

**The cheap experiment: narrow the separations until band discovery breaks, and report the breaking point.** `three_level_hierarchy` exposes `l3_sep`, `l2_sep` and `sigma` as parameters, so the sweep is a loop over one generator at n = 96: hold σ = 0.35 and walk the consecutive-scale ratios down from 5.6× and 7.1× toward 1, recording at each setting how many bands `discover_band_edges` returns and the per-level ARI of each. The code says a threshold exists — a boundary is placed only where a consecutive log-birth gap exceeds both `3.0 ×` the median gap and `min_log_gap` = 0.5, which is a factor of about 1.65 in height — so the experiment is to find where that floor actually bites and whether the failure is graceful (bands merge, coarser level still recovered) or catastrophic (band count collapses to one). Run it over ten seeds, which also supplies the spread Table 5.2 currently lacks. The deliverable is a single figure: recovered levels against scale ratio, with the breaking point marked. That turns the band-separation assumption from something the chapter merely declares into a **measured limit of the method**, which is worth more to the dissertation than the model-based replacement would be, and it is what I would want in hand before anyone asks how separated "well separated" has to be.

## 5.6 Discussion and Contributions

The position I am staking out is narrow and, I think, defensible: a density-free, connectivity-based generator of fuzzy membership functions for problems that arrive as a dissimilarity matrix — the missing bridge from the VAT/iVAT structure I can already compute at scale to the fuzzy antecedents I need. Everything the method needs is a merge height the hierarchy already computed, which is what lets it work with no coordinates and no assumed shape. I have to state that last clause as what it is: a property of the construction, verified by reading the code, not yet a measured result. Every dataset scored in §5.4 was generated from coordinates, and the one coordinate-free experiment measures NERFCM rather than my selector.

I have tried to be equally clear about the boundaries, and several of them got worse when I checked the results file line by line against the prose. The result is carried by the transform, not by the selection machinery. On the battery, plain $k$-means given $k$ ties the field on three of four datasets and fails only the rings — so the non-convexity argument that motivates the chapter is demonstrated by the rings, not by the battery as a whole. My selection gate is deliberately conservative, and on the bridge it does not merely score badly, it discovers the wrong count (3 against 2, at 53% coverage) while both persistence competitors discover the right one; across the four labeled datasets they get $k$ right four times against my three. That failure is narrower than I first framed it in one respect: the multi-scale selector, run on the same bridged data, returns $k$ = 2 at ARI 1.000, so the trade-off belongs to the flat gate rather than to the pipeline — and the two runs have not yet been compared under one scoring, which is why I report the flat result and disclose the other. The trade-off is also a gradient rather than a dichotomy: bottleneck-bootstrap repairs the bridge *and* declines three-quarters of the noise, closer to my gate's behaviour than to beta-plateau's, so there is one competitor currently better on both axes at once. The multi-scale headline is a structural result — one partition becomes $L$, each correct at its own granularity — and not the accuracy gain the averaged column suggests; most of that gap is the aggregation. The falsification experiment I ran to keep the varying-density claim honest is inconclusive as run, because nothing in its recorded output moves with the swept parameter. The band discovery assumes separated scales, is ill-posed when they overlap, and is demonstrated on 96 points at one seed with the scales 5.6× and 7.1× apart. The coverage floor can drop a small real cluster. Every result so far is on synthetic data, and every metric so far is a clustering proxy for the fuzzy-model quality I actually care about. And the overlap with Bonis and Oudot is close enough that it has to be actively managed rather than waved away — which is why the distinction rests on three separate axes, the third of which is the one I would defend if pressed.

None of those limitations is fatal, and stating them is not hedging. This is proposed work; the honest measure of it is whether the open problems are the interesting ones, and I think nested structure in relational data and one-pass membership generation are exactly that.
