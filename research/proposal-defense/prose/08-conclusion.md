# Chapter 8 — Conclusion

I began with a small purring animal and a large practical problem. The tribble reproduces until it fills the ship; a fuzzy inference system, built the usual way, does the same thing with its rule base, and is slow and initialization-sensitive to train besides. The wager of this dissertation is that we can have the good version of that growth — a fuzzy model that scales cleanly from hundreds of samples to hundreds of thousands — without the bad version, and without giving up the readability that made fuzzy models worth using in the first place.

The organizing principle is *structure before search*. Most of the expensive stochastic optimization thrown at fuzzy models is spent rediscovering structure that is already present in the data. If that structure is recovered cheaply and exactly first — where the clusters are, how they nest, how dense they are, which features separate them — then the model largely builds itself, and whatever optimization remains is a cheap local polish rather than the engine that has to find the answer from nothing. Every chapter is an instance of this idea.

What is already established stands on its own. Chapter 3 presented mergeVAT, an exact VAT and iVAT engine that is memory-lean, correct on arbitrary and non-metric dissimilarities, and able to run on datasets that were simply out of reach before — moving the feasible size from a few thousand points to well over a hundred thousand on a single workstation. I have been careful there to rest that claim on reachable problem size rather than on a speedup ratio, because a ratio is only a statement about the baseline it was measured against. Chapter 4 presented a Mixture-of-Gaussians construction that builds an interpretable fuzzy classifier or regressor directly from the data, answer-first, with no genetic algorithm and no gradient descent *in the construction* — the optional antecedent refinement of Chapter 6 is a gradient-based solve, and the best flat figures this document quotes include it, so the claim is about how the model is built rather than about the whole pipeline — training models on hundred-thousand-row datasets in seconds, at an accuracy that matches CART on the regression benchmark and trails a random forest. What that comparison is still missing is a *fuzzy* baseline: no ANFIS or GA-tuned system is measured anywhere in this document, so the "faster than" in the speed claim has nothing yet on the other side of it, and Chapter 7 names the comparison against the methods this construction displaces as the first experiment it owes. The same chapter also synthesizes, as a consequence of building the model out of explicit rules, a complement rule that flags rare and unseen conditions while still being able to say why it fired. That is a validated *mechanism* rather than a measured advantage: the boost knob behaves monotonically exactly as designed and needs no second model, while on the one small dataset available for the test the rule's detection performance is level with an isolation forest. These two chapters are done — Chapter 3's work is published, Chapter 4's paper is in preparation — and defensible independently of everything proposed.

What the proposed work adds is the connective tissue and the reach — and I should say plainly that the connective tissue is the part that is genuinely unfinished, not merely unpolished. Chapter 5 is the bridge I am most excited about: generating fuzzy membership functions directly from the topological structure of a dissimilarity matrix, with the cluster count and even the number of scales as outputs rather than inputs, for the many problems that arrive with no coordinates and no blob-shaped structure to fit. Chapter 6 supplies the hierarchy and the shared closed-form solver that turn those antecedents into full models, trading a little accuracy for an explicit, readable decision structure over named variables. The harness measures one side of that trade and not the other: the accuracy cost is measured, under one protocol, on identical splits and seeds, and the readability it is supposed to buy is still described rather than quantified — Table 6.3's counts are the pending row, and Goal G6 is where they get measured. I would rather say that than let "trade" imply both halves have been weighed. And Chapter 7 lays out the capstone — the whole pipeline running end to end, from a bare dissimilarity matrix to a working fuzzy inference system, measured across the entire path. That last link is the one I have specified but not yet demonstrated: the membership functions of Chapter 5 have not yet been consumed by the models of Chapter 6, and until they are, the pipeline is an argument rather than a result. It is also, for that reason, the most interesting work remaining.

Before the closing argument, the live limits, stated as limits rather than left to be discovered. Three matter most. **There is no fuzzy baseline in this document.** The title and Chapters 1, 7 and this one claim speed against the methods a structure-first construction displaces, and ANFIS and a GA-tuned FIS are the two that would establish it; neither is measured, and until they are, the speed claim is measured only against tree ensembles that were never the target. **The coordinate-free claim rests on synthetic data.** Chapter 3's niche and Chapter 5's premise are both that these methods work where there are no coordinates, and the non-metric matrices tested so far were built *from* coordinate data; Goal G2 is what turns a structural argument into an empirical one, and until it runs, that argument is structural. **The interpretability payoff is described, not quantified.** I have used the word freely and Table 6.3 has an unmeasured row where its counts should be; Goal G6 owes rule counts, path lengths and either an established metric or a small expert study. Three smaller things belong in the same list, because each costs the document something: the timing and accuracy tables have been re-taken on one identified host, but the hardware half of the repeatability protocol — pinned clocks, pinned thermals, a datacenter card with full double-precision throughput — has not been run; Table 3.4's GPU speedup rows are being re-quoted, because one of them turned out to compare two *formulations* rather than two devices, and the exactness result beside it is the part of that table that reproduces; and one goal was refuted, one crossover retracted, two Chapter 3 claims withdrawn as properties of a laptop, and one model found to diverge on one split in ten, all in the course of building the reproduction harness. I count that last as evidence the standard is working rather than as an embarrassment, but a reader is entitled to see the tally in one place.

The broader point I want to leave the committee with is methodological. The prevailing instinct in machine learning is to make the model flexible and let a powerful optimizer find the parameters. That works, but it is expensive, opaque, and fragile to initialization. For the class of problems where interpretability is not optional — regulated domains, safety engineering, expert-in-the-loop settings, and the aerospace applications this department cares about — I think the better instinct is the older one: understand the structure of the data first, and let that structure carry most of the modeling. This dissertation is an argument that *structure before search* is not a compromise for interpretable models but the right way to build them, and that when you do, the tribbles multiply exactly as far as you want them to.
