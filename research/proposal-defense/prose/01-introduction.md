# Chapter 1 — Introduction

## 1.1 Prelude

The tribble is a small, purring creature from *Star Trek* whose only notable talent is reproduction. Left alone with enough food, a handful of tribbles becomes a storage compartment full of tribbles, and eventually a problem for the whole ship. I have named my software after them for two reasons. A Fuzzy Inference System, left to grow naively, has exactly this property: its rule base multiplies until it fills the room. I also want the good version of the same trick: take a fuzzy model that works on a few hundred samples and let it reproduce, cleanly, up to a few hundred thousand.

Fuzzy Inference Systems (FIS) are worth scaling because they are one of the few genuinely interpretable model families. A trained FIS is a short list of IF–THEN rules over linguistic terms (*if the temperature is high and the pressure is low, then risk is elevated*), and a domain expert can read them, disagree, and hand-edit them. Given enough rules and membership functions a FIS can approximate any continuous function to arbitrary accuracy, which establishes that the family is not intrinsically weak. It does not establish that *this* work keeps that capability: the theorem is asymptotic in the rule count, and I work deliberately at the other end of that axis, on the order of one rule per output class, and three output buckets in Chapter 4. Measurement has to answer that question instead. On Concrete, at ten seeds under one protocol, the flat model reaches $R^2 = 0.861 \pm 0.026$ with a full second-order consequent basis, against CART's $0.826 \pm 0.047$ and a random forest's $0.909 \pm 0.019$: level with the tree, behind the forest. At zeroth order, with constant consequents, it scores $0.394 \pm 0.065$: usable, and less than half as good. Capability at a readable rule count is real, and it is not free. Regulators and safety engineers are increasingly unwilling to accept a black box. That makes the combination valuable.

FIS are slow and awkward to build at scale, for three well-understood reasons. The first is rule-base explosion. The number of rules in a naive grid-partitioned FIS is the product of the membership-function counts on each input,

$$ N_{rules} = \prod_{i=1}^{M} N_{\mu_i}, $$

for $M$ input features with $N_{\mu_i}$ fuzzy sets on feature $i$, exponential in the input dimension. A dozen inputs with three fuzzy sets each is already more than half a million rules. No human is going to read half a million rules. The second reason is the way we usually train these models. The dominant methods (genetic algorithms, gradient descent, and ANFIS-style hybrids) are stochastic, slow to converge, and acutely sensitive to their starting point: a genetic algorithm gets there eventually but slowly; gradient descent is much faster only if handed a very good initial guess, otherwise settling into a poor local minimum and reporting it confidently. The third reason is scale. The tools we reach for to understand a dataset before modeling it, cluster tendency and distance structure, were designed for a few thousand points; real datasets now arrive with tens or hundreds of thousands of rows. The Visual Assessment of cluster Tendency (VAT), which I lean on heavily here, is a good example: in its textbook form it is $O(N^3)$, unusable well before the interesting datasets start.

Consider a concrete case: a NASA space-shuttle reentry telemetry set I return to throughout, roughly 58,000 samples across seven sensor channels, about 80% of them in a single flight condition. Before fitting any model I would like to simply *look* at the structure and confirm one dominant regime and a handful of smaller ones. A full VAT image of that data is a 58,000 × 58,000 matrix, about 13 gigabytes at single precision to hold once, and the classical implementation keeps two or three copies. Worse, scaling the cubic algorithm up from a size I can actually time (124 seconds at 4,096 points, measured in Chapter 3) puts 58,000 points at roughly four days. The structure is sitting right there, and the standard toolchain cannot afford to show it to me. With the method in Chapter 3 the same ordering takes about a minute.

This dissertation is built on a single observation I will call *structure before search*: most of the expensive stochastic optimization we throw at fuzzy models is spent rediscovering structure already present in the data. If I can recover that structure cheaply (where the clusters are, how they nest, how dense they are, which features separate them) the model almost builds itself: the membership functions come from the shape of the data, the rules from how the clusters relate to the labels, and whatever optimization remains is a cheap local polish. The claim of this work is that a structure-first pipeline produces fuzzy models whose training cost is a second or two at the sizes it reaches, with no stochastic search in the fit at all, that scale from hundreds to hundreds of thousands of samples, and that stay interpretable by construction. I state that as an absolute, not a comparison, on purpose. The comparison a reader will want, this pipeline against ANFIS and a genetic-algorithm-tuned FIS on identical splits, is Goal G3, checklist item C1; those two adapters are not in the repository, so every ANFIS and GA-FIS cell in Table 4.5 reads `N/A`. There is no measured conventional baseline anywhere in this document to be faster *than*, so I quote seconds and not speedup ratios.

**Figure 1.1 — The two routes to a trained FIS.** Left, the conventional route: grid-partition every input, then grind a genetic algorithm or gradient descent against the whole model. Right, the structure-first route: recover the structure, read the membership functions and rules off it, treat the polish as optional. ## 1.2 Unique Contributions

The dissertation is organized as a pipeline, and each stage is a contribution. Every individual component I use (VAT, iVAT, single-linkage clustering, Fuzzy C-Means, persistence-based clustering, minimax linkage, the mixture-of-experts architecture and its EM, Lin–Kernighan local search, and the Takagi–Sugeno–Kang rule form) is prior art, and I credit it as such, including the cases where a competitor sits uncomfortably close to what I claim. What is new is how the pieces compose into a fast, scalable, interpretable modeling pipeline, and the specific regimes that composition reaches which the existing methods do not.

1. **mergeVAT (priority-queue VAT): exact VAT and iVAT at scale.** The feasible problem size moves from a few thousand points to well over a hundred thousand, on ordinary hardware, with the result bit-identical to the serial reference. Three things get it there. The reorder improves on the literature's cubic implementation in two stages: to $O(N^2 \log N)$ by replacing its redundant re-scanning with a priority queue, the published result; then to $O(N^2)$, because the reorder only ever needs the current minimum, so a compact active set with relaxation and selection fused into one pass removes the heap and the log factor together. That second stage is unpublished; Chapter 9 records what a write-up could defensibly claim. The memory scheme holds the computation in a single matrix instead of the usual two or three, permuting it in place. That is what actually lifts the size ceiling: by $\sqrt{3}$ over the classical scheme, and another $\sqrt{2}$ at single precision, which Chapter 3 shows costs nothing in exactness. A divide-and-conquer scheme splits problems larger than one machine, stitching the pieces back at bounded cost. The underlying minimum spanning tree also runs on a GPU. The engine also carries a clustering method, `IVATMeans`: deterministic and initialization-free, returning assignment and membership from one fit and verifiable against the reordered image it read them off, with the head-to-head against Fuzzy C-Means and k-means owed (Goal G9). Throughout, the method requires only an arbitrary dissimilarity matrix and assumes no metric. The coordinate-based fast-VAT variants cannot enter that regime at all.

2. **Fast interpretable FIS synthesis from Mixtures of Gaussians.** I generate the membership functions *and* the rules of a fuzzy classifier or regressor directly from the data, using per-feature Gaussian mixtures, with no genetic algorithm and no gradient descent afterward. Because the rules are built per output class instead of by gridding the inputs, the rule base does not explode: a $K$-class problem produces on the order of $K$ rules. On the 235,000-row PhiUSIIL phishing set this trains a two-rule classifier to $0.997 \pm 0.001$ accuracy in $0.64 \pm 0.02$ seconds, ten seeds, in the run of record. The accuracy is the less interesting half: CART and a random forest both score a perfect $1.000$ on the same data, so PhiUSIIL is saturated and cannot separate these methods on accuracy at all. What the row demonstrates is the rule count and the training time. The scale target is RT-IOT2022 (123,000 samples, 83 features, 12 classes), where the construction predicts twelve rules against a grid form past enumeration. That dataset is not in the repository, so twelve rules is a count derived from $K$, not a training time I have measured; Table 4.4 marks the row unrun rather than filling it. One consequence I did not set out to obtain, then built deliberately once I saw it: because every class is an explicit fuzzy rule, the complement of their aggregate is automatically a *none of the above* rule. That turns the classifier into an open-set detector for rare and never-before-seen conditions at no additional training cost, one that can still say *why* it fired.

3. **Membership functions from topological structure** *(proposed, with preliminary results)*. Given only a dissimilarity matrix, no coordinates and no Gaussian assumption, I extract fuzzy membership functions, the number of clusters, and even the number of *scales* directly from the minimax (single-linkage) hierarchy, using a persistence-gated set-cover in which the cluster count is an output, not an input. It recovers nested structure that a flat method cannot represent. A flat cover returns one partition, so on a synthetic hierarchy with two or three true levels it lands one of them exactly (adjusted Rand index 1.000 on the coarse level in all three sets) and necessarily misses the rest, scoring 0.24 to 0.49 on the levels it cannot represent. The band stack returns one partition per true level and scores 1.000 on each. Table 5.2's flat column of 0.58–0.75 is that single partition averaged over all the levels, so the gap is a difference in what the two methods can *return* and not an accuracy lift from 0.58 to 1.00. This is the bridge between the clustering and fuzzy-modeling work, and the part I consider most novel, with the caveat Chapter 5 makes explicit: its results so far are clustering scores, not end-to-end fuzzy-model accuracy.

4. **Hierarchical and refined fuzzy models** *(partly built, partly proposed)*. One closed-form, firing-weighted ridge least-squares solver serves as a shared primitive across a flat FIS, a soft fuzzy decision tree, and a hierarchical mixture of fuzzy experts, and I export the result to an explicit triangular (Ruspini) rule base a person can read and edit. The interpretability-versus-accuracy trade also becomes explicit here. It is not favorable. Measured under one protocol, the hierarchy does not *beat* the flat model on Concrete; tuned, the two are level within their spreads, 0.833 against 0.861. What the hierarchy buys is an explicit decision path over named variables, not accuracy.

5. **A supporting optimization engine.** Underneath the pipeline sits a general optimization library (metaheuristics, a dual-backend Lin–Kernighan local search, a quality-diversity layer, high-performance kernels) providing the optional local-polish stage. I treat it as infrastructure, not a headline result; it lives in an appendix, and the point of the dissertation is precisely that this engine is *not* on the critical path.

The claim is not that any one of these primitives is new, but that arranging them structure-first lets a fuzzy model reproduce like a tribble, cleanly and across orders of magnitude of dataset size, without losing the readability that made it worth building.

## 1.3 Dissertation Outline

The remainder follows the pipeline in order, separating work already done from work proposed.

**Part I — Introduction and Preliminaries.** Chapter 2 collects the shared background: fuzzy inference systems and the Takagi–Sugeno–Kang rule form; VAT, iVAT, and their equivalence to single-linkage clustering; persistence and a little topological data analysis; Fuzzy C-Means and relational fuzzy clustering; why the literature treats stochastic optimization as the bottleneck (the foil for *structure before search*); and the accuracy–interpretability trade-off.

**Part II — Completed Work.** Chapter 3 presents mergeVAT: the acceleration, the in-place memory scheme, the GPU implementation, the divide-and-conquer stitch, and the extension to non-metric data, along with the VAT-to-TSP hot-start from the same machinery. This work was presented at NAFIPS 2025 (Banff) and NAFIPS 2026 (El Paso); Chapter 9 has the paper-level detail, which is not yet settled. Chapter 4 presents the Mixture-of-Gaussians approach to synthesizing a fuzzy model directly from data: the fast, no-search core of the pipeline.

**Part III — Proposed Work and Goals for Completion.** Chapter 5 is the proposed contribution I am most excited about: generating fuzzy membership functions from the topological structure of a dissimilarity matrix, including the multi-scale case. It has strong preliminary results already, and it is the conceptual bridge from clustering to fuzzy modeling. Chapter 6 covers the hierarchical and refined models: the shared ridge-TSK solver, the fuzzy trees and the mixture of experts, some built and some proposed. Chapter 7 lays out the goals for completion: the integrated end-to-end pipeline, the experiments that would make the scale and accuracy claims airtight, and a risk register with a de-scoping plan. Chapter 8 concludes, Chapter 9 lists publications, Chapter 10 gives the timeline through the final defense.
