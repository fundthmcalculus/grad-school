# Chapter 1 — Introduction

## 1.1 Prelude

The tribble is a small, purring creature from *Star Trek* whose only notable talent is reproduction. Left alone with enough food, a handful of tribbles becomes a storage compartment full of tribbles, and eventually a problem for the whole ship. I have named my software after them for two reasons. The first is that a Fuzzy Inference System, left to grow naively, has exactly this property: its rule base multiplies until it fills the room. The second is that I want the good version of the same trick — I want to take a fuzzy model that works on a few hundred samples and let it reproduce, cleanly, up to a few hundred thousand.

Fuzzy Inference Systems (FIS) are worth scaling because they are one of the few model families that are genuinely interpretable. A trained FIS is a short list of IF–THEN rules written over linguistic terms — *if the temperature is high and the pressure is low, then risk is elevated* — and a domain expert can read those rules, agree or disagree with them, and hand-edit them. Given enough rules and membership functions, a FIS can approximate any continuous function to arbitrary accuracy, which establishes that the family is not intrinsically weak. It does not establish that *this* work keeps that capability, because the theorem is asymptotic in the rule count and I work deliberately at the other end of that axis — on the order of one rule per output class, and three output buckets in Chapter 4. So the capability question has to be answered by measurement rather than by the theorem, and it is: on Concrete, at ten seeds under one protocol, the flat model reaches $R^2 = 0.859 \pm 0.039$ with a full second-order consequent basis, against CART's $0.826 \pm 0.047$ and a random forest's $0.909 \pm 0.019$ — level with the tree, behind the forest. At zeroth order, with constant consequents, the same model scores $-0.334 \pm 0.201$, which is worse than predicting the mean. Capability at a readable rule count is real, and it is not free. In an era where regulators and safety engineers are increasingly unwilling to accept a black box, that combination is valuable.

The trouble is that FIS are slow and awkward to build at scale, and the reasons are well understood. The first is rule-base explosion. The number of rules in a naive grid-partitioned FIS is the product of the number of membership functions on each input,

$$ N_{rules} = \prod_{i=1}^{M} N_{\mu_i}, $$

for $M$ input features with $N_{\mu_i}$ fuzzy sets on feature $i$ — exponential in the input dimension. A dozen inputs with three fuzzy sets each is already more than half a million rules, and no human is going to read half a million rules. The second reason is the way we usually train these models. The dominant methods — genetic algorithms, gradient descent, and ANFIS-style hybrids — are stochastic and slow to converge, and they are acutely sensitive to their starting point. A genetic algorithm will find a decent solution eventually, but it spends a great deal of time getting there. Gradient descent is much faster, but only if it is handed a very good initial guess; otherwise it settles into a poor local minimum and reports it confidently. The third reason is simply scale. The tools we normally reach for to understand a dataset before modeling it — cluster tendency, distance structure — were designed for a few thousand points, and real datasets now arrive with tens or hundreds of thousands of rows. The Visual Assessment of cluster Tendency (VAT), which I lean on heavily in this work, is a good example: in its textbook form it is an $O(N^3)$ algorithm, and it becomes unusable well before the interesting datasets start.

Consider a concrete case. One of the datasets I return to throughout this proposal is a NASA space-shuttle reentry telemetry set: roughly 58,000 samples across seven sensor channels, with about 80% of the records sitting in a single flight condition. Before I fit any model, I would like to simply *look* at the structure — confirm that there is one dominant regime and a handful of smaller ones. A full VAT image of that data is a 58,000 × 58,000 matrix, which at single precision is about 13 gigabytes just to hold once, and the classical implementation keeps two or three copies. Worse, scaling the cubic algorithm up from a size where I can actually time it — 124 seconds at 4,096 points, measured in Chapter 3 — puts 58,000 points at roughly four days of computation. The structure is sitting right there in the data, and the standard toolchain cannot afford to show it to me. With the method in Chapter 3 the same ordering takes about a minute.

This dissertation is built on a single observation, which I will call *structure before search*: most of the expensive stochastic optimization we throw at fuzzy models is spent rediscovering structure that is already present in the data. If I can recover that structure cheaply — where the clusters are, how they nest, how dense they are, which features separate them — then the model almost builds itself. The membership functions come from the shape of the data, the rules come from how the clusters relate to the labels, and whatever optimization remains is a cheap local polish rather than the main event. The claim of this work is that a structure-first pipeline produces fuzzy models whose training cost is a second or two at the sizes it reaches, with no stochastic search in the fit at all, that scale from hundreds to hundreds of thousands of samples, and that stay interpretable by construction. I state that as an absolute rather than as a comparison on purpose. The comparison a reader will want — this pipeline against ANFIS and a genetic-algorithm-tuned FIS on identical splits — is Goal G3, tracked as checklist item C1, and those two adapters are not in the repository, so every ANFIS and GA-FIS cell in Table 4.5 reads `N/A`. There is no measured conventional baseline anywhere in this document to be faster *than*, and I would rather quote the seconds I have taken than a ratio I have not.

**Figure 1.1 — The two routes to a trained FIS.** On the left the conventional route: grid-partition every input, then grind a genetic algorithm or gradient descent against the whole model. On the right the structure-first route: recover the structure, read the membership functions and rules off it, and treat the polish as optional. The two readings underneath are the contrast this section is drawing. The rule counts are exact arithmetic rather than measurements — the grid product $\prod_i N_{\mu_i}$ evaluated on PhiUSIIL's 54 features, against the $K$ rules the answer-first construction of Chapter 4 produces on the same data. The training times are deliberately *not* symmetric: the structure-first number is measured and read from Table 4.1, and the conventional side has no measured baseline anywhere in this document, because the ANFIS and GA-FIS adapters are Goal G3. ## 1.2 Unique Contributions

The dissertation is organized as a pipeline, and each stage is a contribution. I want to be honest at the outset about what is and is not new. Every individual component I use — VAT, iVAT, single-linkage clustering, Fuzzy C-Means, persistence-based clustering, minimax linkage, the mixture-of-experts architecture and its EM, Lin–Kernighan local search, and the Takagi–Sugeno–Kang rule form — is prior art, and I credit it as such, including the cases where a competitor sits uncomfortably close to what I claim. What is new is the way the pieces are composed into a fast, scalable, interpretable modeling pipeline, and the specific regimes that composition reaches which the existing methods do not. The contributions are as follows.

1. **mergeVAT (priority-queue VAT): exact VAT and iVAT at scale.** The headline is what becomes computable: the feasible problem size moves from a few thousand points to well over a hundred thousand, on ordinary hardware, with the result bit-identical to the serial reference. Three things get it there. The reorder improves on the literature's cubic implementation in two stages: first to $O(N^2 \log N)$ by replacing its redundant re-scanning with a priority queue, which is the published result; then to $O(N^2)$ by observing that the queue is unnecessary, since the reorder only ever needs the current minimum — a compact active set with the relaxation and selection fused into one pass removes the heap and the log factor together. That second stage is unpublished; Chapter 9 records what a write-up could defensibly claim. The memory scheme holds the computation in a single matrix rather than the usual two or three, permuting it in place, and this is what actually lifts the size ceiling — by a factor of $\sqrt{3}$ over the classical scheme, and another $\sqrt{2}$ at single precision, which Chapter 3 shows costs nothing in exactness. And a divide-and-conquer scheme splits problems larger than one machine, stitching the pieces back at bounded cost. The underlying minimum spanning tree also runs on a GPU. Throughout, the method requires only an arbitrary dissimilarity matrix — it does not assume a metric, which is the regime the coordinate-based fast-VAT variants cannot enter at all.

2. **Fast interpretable FIS synthesis from Mixtures of Gaussians.** I generate the membership functions *and* the rules of a fuzzy classifier or regressor directly from the data, using per-feature Gaussian mixtures, with no genetic algorithm and no gradient descent afterward. Because the rules are built per output class rather than by gridding the inputs, the rule base does not explode — a $K$-class problem produces on the order of $K$ rules. On the 235,000-row PhiUSIIL phishing set this trains a two-rule classifier to $0.997 \pm 0.001$ accuracy in $0.64 \pm 0.02$ seconds, ten seeds, in the run of record — and the accuracy is the less interesting half of that sentence, because CART and a random forest both score a perfect $1.000$ on the same data. PhiUSIIL is saturated and cannot separate these methods on accuracy at all; what the row demonstrates is the rule count and the training time. The scale target is RT-IOT2022 — 123,000 samples, 83 features, 12 classes — where the construction predicts twelve rules against a grid form that is past enumeration. That dataset is not in the repository, so twelve rules is a count I can derive from $K$ and not a training time I have measured, and Table 4.4 marks the row as unrun rather than filling it. The same construction yields a consequence I did not set out to obtain and then built deliberately once I saw it was available: because every class is an explicit fuzzy rule, taking the complement of their aggregate produces a *none of the above* rule automatically, which turns the classifier into an open-set detector for rare and never-before-seen conditions at no additional training cost — and one that can still say *why* it fired.

3. **Membership functions from topological structure** *(proposed, with preliminary results)*. Given only a dissimilarity matrix — no coordinates, no Gaussian assumption — I extract fuzzy membership functions, the number of clusters, and even the number of *scales* directly from the minimax (single-linkage) hierarchy, using a persistence-gated set-cover in which the cluster count is an output rather than an input. It recovers nested structure that a flat method cannot represent, and the difference is structural rather than a difference in accuracy. A flat cover returns one partition, so on a synthetic hierarchy with two or three true levels it lands one of them exactly — adjusted Rand index 1.000 on the coarse level in all three sets — and necessarily misses the rest, scoring 0.24 to 0.49 on the levels it was never able to represent. The band stack returns one partition per true level and scores 1.000 on each. Table 5.2's flat column of 0.58–0.75 is that single partition averaged over all the levels, so I would rather describe the gap as a difference in what the two methods can *return* than quote it as an accuracy lift from 0.58 to 1.00. This is the bridge between the clustering work and the fuzzy-modeling work, and the part I consider most novel — with the caveat, which Chapter 5 makes explicit, that its results so far are clustering scores rather than end-to-end fuzzy-model accuracy.

4. **Hierarchical and refined fuzzy models** *(partly built, partly proposed)*. I use one closed-form, firing-weighted ridge least-squares solver as a shared primitive across a flat FIS, a soft fuzzy decision tree, and a hierarchical mixture of fuzzy experts, and I export the result to an explicit triangular (Ruspini) rule base that a person can read and edit. A memory-augmented variant extends the same machinery to time, and on a double pendulum the one-step-ahead error falls by something of the order of a third — which I record as a demonstration rather than a contribution, because that script runs at one fixed seed outside the harness and emits no CSV, its two rows imply inconsistent target scales, and the iterated rollout that would make it a *trajectory* result returns its initial window unchanged today (Chapter 6 §6.3.6; checklist C7). This is also where I make the interpretability-versus-accuracy trade explicit rather than favorable: measured under one protocol the hierarchy does not *beat* the flat model on Concrete — tuned, the two are level within their spreads, 0.833 against 0.861 — and what it buys is an explicit decision path over named variables rather than accuracy. I would rather state that plainly than let it be discovered.

5. **A supporting optimization engine.** Underneath the pipeline sits a general optimization library — metaheuristics, a dual-backend Lin–Kernighan local search, a quality-diversity layer, and a set of high-performance kernels — that provides the optional local-polish stage. I treat it deliberately as infrastructure rather than as a headline result; it lives in an appendix, and the point of the dissertation is precisely that this engine is *not* on the critical path.

Taken together, the claim is not that any one of these primitives is new, but that arranging them structure-first lets a fuzzy model reproduce like a tribble — cleanly, and across orders of magnitude of dataset size — without losing the readability that made it worth building in the first place.

## 1.3 Dissertation Outline

The remainder of the document follows the pipeline in order, and separates work that is already done from work that is proposed.

**Part I — Introduction and Preliminaries.** Chapter 2 collects the background that later chapters share: fuzzy inference systems and the Takagi–Sugeno–Kang rule form; VAT, iVAT, and their equivalence to single-linkage clustering; a light treatment of persistence and topological data analysis; Fuzzy C-Means and relational fuzzy clustering; a brief account of why the literature treats stochastic optimization as the bottleneck (the foil for *structure before search*); and the accuracy–interpretability trade-off.

**Part II — Completed Work.** Chapter 3 presents mergeVAT: the acceleration, the in-place memory scheme, the GPU implementation, the divide-and-conquer stitch, and the extension to non-metric data, along with the VAT-to-TSP hot-start that came out of the same machinery. This work was published across NAFIPS 2025 (Banff) and NAFIPS 2026 (El Paso). Chapter 4 presents the Mixture-of-Gaussians approach to synthesizing a fuzzy model directly from data, which is the fast, no-search core of the modeling pipeline.

**Part III — Proposed Work and Goals for Completion.** Chapter 5 is the proposed contribution I am most excited about: generating fuzzy membership functions from the topological structure of a dissimilarity matrix, including the multi-scale case. It already has strong preliminary results, and it is the conceptual bridge from clustering to fuzzy modeling. Chapter 6 covers the hierarchical and refined models — the shared ridge-TSK solver, the fuzzy trees and mixture of experts, and the memory-augmented variant — some of which is built and some of which is proposed. Chapter 7 lays out the goals for completion: the integrated end-to-end pipeline, the experiments needed to make the scale and accuracy claims airtight, and an honest risk register with a de-scoping plan. Chapter 8 concludes, Chapter 9 lists publications, and Chapter 10 gives the timeline through the final defense.

**Figure 1.2 — The end-to-end pipeline as a roadmap.** Raw data through structure discovery (Ch. 3), membership generation (Ch. 5), FIS synthesis (Ch. 4 and Ch. 6) and an optional refinement (Ch. 6, App. A) to an interpretable fuzzy model, with each stage's claim beside it. Every one of those claims is read at draw time from the CSV of the table that owns it — the speedup from Table 3.1, the multi-scale ARI from Table 5.2, the accuracy and training time from Table 4.1, refinement's decay from the Concrete reconciliation — so the roadmap cannot advertise a number the tables no longer support. Refinement is drawn dashed on purpose: the claim of this work is that the search everyone else puts on the critical path is not on this one.
