# Chapter 1 — Introduction

## 1.1 Prelude

The tribble is a small, purring creature from *Star Trek* whose only notable talent is reproduction. Left alone with enough food, a handful of tribbles becomes a storage compartment full of tribbles, and eventually a problem for the whole ship. I have named my software after them for two reasons. The first is that a Fuzzy Inference System, left to grow naively, has exactly this property: its rule base multiplies until it fills the room. The second is that I want the good version of the same trick — I want to take a fuzzy model that works on a few hundred samples and let it reproduce, cleanly, up to a few hundred thousand.

Fuzzy Inference Systems (FIS) are worth scaling because they are one of the few model families that are genuinely interpretable. A trained FIS is a short list of IF–THEN rules written over linguistic terms — *if the temperature is high and the pressure is low, then risk is elevated* — and a domain expert can read those rules, agree or disagree with them, and hand-edit them. Given enough rules and membership functions, a FIS can approximate any continuous function to arbitrary accuracy, so we are not trading away capability to get that readability. In an era where regulators and safety engineers are increasingly unwilling to accept a black box, that combination is valuable.

The trouble is that FIS are slow and awkward to build at scale, and the reasons are well understood. The first is rule-base explosion. The number of rules in a naive grid-partitioned FIS is the product of the number of membership functions on each input,

$$ N_{rules} = \prod_{i=1}^{M} N_{\mu_i}, $$

for $M$ input features with $N_{\mu_i}$ fuzzy sets on feature $i$ — exponential in the input dimension. A dozen inputs with three fuzzy sets each is already more than half a million rules, and no human is going to read half a million rules. The second reason is the way we usually train these models. The dominant methods — genetic algorithms, gradient descent, and ANFIS-style hybrids — are stochastic and slow to converge, and they are acutely sensitive to their starting point. A genetic algorithm will find a decent solution eventually, but it spends a great deal of time getting there. Gradient descent is much faster, but only if it is handed a very good initial guess; otherwise it settles into a poor local minimum and reports it confidently. The third reason is simply scale. The tools we normally reach for to understand a dataset before modeling it — cluster tendency, distance structure — were designed for a few thousand points, and real datasets now arrive with tens or hundreds of thousands of rows. The Visual Assessment of cluster Tendency (VAT), which I lean on heavily in this work, is a good example: in its textbook form it is an $O(N^3)$ algorithm, and it becomes unusable well before the interesting datasets start.

Consider a concrete case. One of the datasets I return to throughout this proposal is a NASA space-shuttle reentry telemetry set: roughly 58,000 samples across seven sensor channels, with about 80% of the records sitting in a single flight condition. Before I fit any model, I would like to simply *look* at the structure — confirm that there is one dominant regime and a handful of smaller ones. A full VAT image of that data is a 58,000 × 58,000 matrix; at single-precision that is well over ten gigabytes just to hold it, and the classical algorithm would take the better part of an afternoon to order it. The structure is right there in the data, but the standard toolchain cannot afford to show it to me.

This dissertation is built on a single observation, which I will call *structure before search*: most of the expensive stochastic optimization we throw at fuzzy models is spent rediscovering structure that is already present in the data. If I can recover that structure cheaply — where the clusters are, how they nest, how dense they are, which features separate them — then the model almost builds itself. The membership functions come from the shape of the data, the rules come from how the clusters relate to the labels, and whatever optimization remains is a cheap local polish rather than the main event. The claim of this work is that a structure-first pipeline produces fuzzy models that train orders of magnitude faster, scale from hundreds to hundreds of thousands of samples, and stay interpretable by construction.

## 1.2 Unique Contributions

The dissertation is organized as a pipeline, and each stage is a contribution. I want to be honest at the outset about what is and is not new. Every individual component I use — VAT, iVAT, single-linkage clustering, Fuzzy C-Means, persistence, Lin–Kernighan local search, the Takagi–Sugeno–Kang rule form — is prior art, and I credit it as such. What is new is the way the pieces are composed into a fast, scalable, interpretable modeling pipeline, and the specific regimes that composition reaches which the existing methods do not. The contributions are as follows.

1. **pVAT (priority-queue VAT): exact VAT and iVAT at scale.** I reduce VAT and iVAT from $O(N^3)$ to $O(N^2 \log N)$, hold the whole computation in a single matrix rather than the usual two or three, run the underlying minimum spanning tree on a GPU while producing a result that is bit-identical to the serial reference, and split large problems with a divide-and-conquer scheme that stitches the pieces back together at bounded cost. Crucially, the method works on an arbitrary — including non-metric — dissimilarity matrix, a regime the fast VAT variants in the literature cannot touch. In practice this moves the feasible problem size from a few thousand points to well over a hundred thousand.

2. **Fast interpretable FIS synthesis from Mixtures of Gaussians.** I generate the membership functions *and* the rules of a fuzzy classifier or regressor directly from the data, using per-feature Gaussian mixtures, with no genetic algorithm and no gradient descent afterward. Because the rules are built per output class rather than by gridding the inputs, the rule base does not explode — a $K$-class problem produces on the order of $K$ rules. This trains a phishing classifier to 97–99% accuracy in about six seconds, and a 123,000-sample, 83-feature, 12-class intrusion dataset in under a minute.

3. **Membership functions from topological structure.** Given only a dissimilarity matrix — no coordinates, no Gaussian assumption — I extract fuzzy membership functions, the number of clusters, and even the number of *scales* directly from the minimax (single-linkage) hierarchy, using a persistence-gated set-cover in which the cluster count is an output rather than an input. This is the bridge between the clustering work and the fuzzy-modeling work, and it is the part I consider the most novel. It recovers nested structure that a flat method cannot represent.

4. **Hierarchical and refined fuzzy models.** I use one closed-form, firing-weighted ridge least-squares solver as a shared primitive across a flat FIS, a soft fuzzy decision tree, and a hierarchical mixture of fuzzy experts, and I export the result to an explicit triangular (Ruspini) rule base. A memory-augmented variant extends the same machinery to dynamical systems. This is where the interpretability-versus-accuracy trade-off is made explicit and, I argue, made honestly.

5. **A supporting optimization engine.** Underneath the pipeline sits a general optimization library — metaheuristics, a dual-backend Lin–Kernighan local search, a quality-diversity layer, and a set of high-performance kernels — that provides the optional local-polish stage. I treat it deliberately as infrastructure rather than as a headline result; it lives in an appendix, and the point of the dissertation is precisely that this engine is *not* on the critical path.

Taken together, the claim is not that any one of these primitives is new, but that arranging them structure-first lets a fuzzy model reproduce like a tribble — cleanly, and by orders of magnitude — without losing the readability that made it worth building in the first place.

## 1.3 Dissertation Outline

The remainder of the document follows the pipeline in order, and separates work that is already done from work that is proposed.

**Part I — Introduction and Preliminaries.** Chapter 2 collects the background that later chapters share: fuzzy inference systems and the Takagi–Sugeno–Kang rule form; VAT, iVAT, and their equivalence to single-linkage clustering; a light treatment of persistence and topological data analysis; Fuzzy C-Means and relational fuzzy clustering; a brief account of why stochastic optimization is the bottleneck (the foil for *structure before search*); and the accuracy–interpretability trade-off.

**Part II — Completed Work.** Chapter 3 presents pVAT: the acceleration, the in-place memory scheme, the GPU implementation, the divide-and-conquer stitch, and the extension to non-metric data, along with the VAT-to-TSP hot-start that came out of the same machinery. This work was published across NAFIPS 2025 (Banff) and NAFIPS 2026 (El Paso). Chapter 4 presents the Mixture-of-Gaussians approach to synthesizing a fuzzy model directly from data, which is the fast, no-search core of the modeling pipeline.

**Part III — Proposed Work and Goals for Completion.** Chapter 5 is the proposed contribution I am most excited about: generating fuzzy membership functions from the topological structure of a dissimilarity matrix, including the multi-scale case. It already has strong preliminary results, and it is the conceptual bridge from clustering to fuzzy modeling. Chapter 6 covers the hierarchical and refined models — the shared ridge-TSK solver, the fuzzy trees and mixture of experts, and the memory-augmented variant — some of which is built and some of which is proposed. Chapter 7 lays out the goals for completion: the integrated end-to-end pipeline, the experiments needed to make the scale and accuracy claims airtight, and an honest risk register with a de-scoping plan. Chapter 8 concludes, Chapter 9 lists publications, and Chapter 10 gives the timeline through the final defense.
